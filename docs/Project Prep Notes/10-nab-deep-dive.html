The NAB platform, properly

What was configured at the edge, what the gateway actually enforced, how the fast path is built, what runs off it, and the wallet work in full — each with the follow-ups that come next. How to use this page

You will not say all of this. You will say the first two sentences of each section, and then answer follow-ups from the rest. That is how a deep answer actually works in an interview — short first, deep on demand. Read a section, close the drop-downs, and say the short version out loud.

Part 1

The shape, in one paragraph

Every question on this page has the same root, so learn the root and the rest follows.

Start here, every time

"The constraint was external and we could not negotiate it. A card authorisation has a scheme-mandated response budget — the issuer answers within a few hundred milliseconds or the transaction times out and goes to stand-in. Fraud got a slice of a budget somebody else set. That one fact splits the design in two: the decision is synchronous because a payment is waiting on it, and everything that is not waiting is asynchronous."

Everything else — the edge rules, the timeouts, the caching, the event stream — is a consequence of that sentence. If you get lost mid-answer, come back to it.

Follow-up: "Why not just make the whole thing asynchronous?"

"Because the authorisation is blocking on the answer. There is no such thing as an eventually-consistent decline — the terminal needs a response while the cardholder is standing there."

Follow-up: "Why not make the whole thing synchronous and keep it simple?"

"Because then every downstream consumer is on the payment's critical path. The moment a reporting consumer can slow an authorisation, you have built a fraud system that declines good customers when the dashboard is busy. The async half is not an optimisation, it is the isolation."

Follow-up: "What does the edge actually protect you from that the gateway doesn't?"

"Volume that is not worth authenticating. Validating a token costs something. Scoring a transaction costs a lot more. The edge exists so that a flood never reaches the part of the system where requests are expensive."

Follow-up: "Rate limiting and concurrency limiting — what's the difference?"

"Rate is requests per second. Concurrency is requests in flight at the same time. They catch different problems: rate stops a client hammering you; concurrency stops a slow dependency turning ten requests a second into a thousand simultaneous open calls. A system can be well inside its rate limit and still be out of threads."

This is a genuinely common distinction to be tested on, and getting it right reads as experience.

Follow-up: "429 or 503 — which do you return, and why does it matter?"

"429 whenever you possibly can. 429 tells one specific client it is over its limit and should back off. 503 tells everyone the platform is in trouble — and in a bank that is a very different conversation, because partners and monitoring both react to it. Returning 429 is a controlled outcome; returning 503 is an incident."

Follow-up: "Would you allow-list partner IP addresses?"

"Where the partner's egress is genuinely static, yes — it is a cheap extra layer. But I would never treat it as the trust anchor. A lot of partner traffic now comes out of cloud egress that is dynamic, shared, or changes without telling you, so address allow-listing becomes a coarse filter that creates outages rather than a control that prevents breaches. The identity has to come from the certificate and the token."

Part 3

The gateway — two different questions, answered separately

This is your strongest single piece of security material, so slow down here. The whole thing rests on one distinction.

The distinction, said exactly

"mTLS answers 'who is on this connection'. OAuth answers 'what is this software allowed to do'. They are different questions, you need both, and neither of them answers 'whose customer record is this' — which is why the object-level check has to live in the service."

What is configuredRoughlyWhat it is actually for

OAuth2 / OIDCShort-lived tokens, validated on every callSays what that software is permitted to do. Validated on every request, not trusted because it was valid at issue — a stale entitlement is a breach, and that is a different failure mode from an expired certificate.

Stage-wide ceiling~5,000/second, burst ~10,000The building's maximum occupancy. Even if every partner is individually inside their own plan, the total is capped, because backend capacity is finite and a coordinated spike is a real scenario.
Per-method limitsThe decision endpoint tighter than everything elseAn expensive endpoint gets a stricter limit than a cheap one. Otherwise your most costly call is your least protected.

The honesty boundary on this one — say it before they find it

The OAuth / mTLS / JWT pattern already existed as a reference design before you arrived. What you did was instantiate it for the fraud, tokenisation and disputes APIs — decide where each control actually enforces, which is the harder half — and other teams then used your instantiation rather than re-deriving it.

Say it like this: "I didn't originate it — there was already a reference design in the bank. What I owned was making it real for a specific estate, and that turned out to be the harder half." That answer survives a follow-up. Claiming authorship does not.

Follow-up: "If you already have mTLS, why do you need OAuth as well?"

"Because mTLS proves a system's identity on the connection and nothing else. It cannot express scope, it cannot expire in minutes, and it cannot be revoked quickly. OAuth gives you narrow permissions, short life, revocation and a decent audit trail. Different problems."

Follow-up: "Where do you terminate TLS?"

"At the edge, so policy is enforced in one place — and then re-encrypt to the backend, so it is still encrypted the rest of the way. Terminating and then running plaintext internally is the thing to avoid, and it is still surprisingly common."

Follow-up: "How do you rotate partner certificates without an outage?"

"Overlap. Register the new certificate, trust both for a controlled window, watch which one is actually being used, then retire the old one. Never a cutover at midnight. And alert on approaching expiry early enough that the partner has time to act, because you are dependent on their change process, not yours."

Follow-up: "What is object-level authorization, and where does it live?"

"It is the check that this record belongs to this caller. The gateway can tell me which client is calling. It can never tell me whose record this is — that needs business context the gateway does not have. So the check has to be in the service, on every read, and the query has to be scoped by the caller's own context rather than by whatever identifier arrived in the request."

Rehearse this until it is automatic. It is the most common serious API flaw in banking, and offering it before being asked is a strong signal.

Follow-up: "Give me a concrete example of it going wrong."

"An endpoint that takes an account identifier in the path, authenticates the caller properly, and then trusts the identifier. The caller is real. The record isn't theirs. It passes every gateway control and every test that only checks authentication. The fix is unglamorous: never query by the identifier alone, always query by the identifier and the caller's own scope."

Part 4

The real-time path — how the fast side is actually built

figure 2 — idempotency first, external calls last and bounded, write before you answer

Why the order is the order

  1. Idempotency first, before any work. The schemes retry. If the same authorisation arrives twice, the second one must return the first one's answer, not produce a second answer. Two different decisions for one transaction is not a performance problem, it is an explainability problem — you cannot tell a regulator which one was real.
  2. Enrichment is a lookup, never a calculation. A rule like "how many transactions has this card attempted in ten minutes, across how many countries" cannot be computed from a cold database inside the budget. So those counters are precomputed, held in a fast store, and kept current by the event stream. The events feed the state that the fast path reads.
  3. External calls last, bounded, and skippable. Each vendor gets its own adapter with its own timeout and its own breaker, so vendor-specific behaviour never leaks into the core logic.
  4. Rules before model. Deterministic rules are fast and explainable and handle the common case; the behavioural score is layered on only if it fits in the remaining budget.
  5. Write, then answer. The decision is persisted with a conditional write on the key before the response goes back, so a retry cannot race it.
Follow-up: "How exactly do you implement idempotency?"

"Key on the domain identifier — the authorisation identifier — not on anything the transport invented, because a message ID changes when a retry comes through a different route. Then claim the key with a conditional write: the first one succeeds, and any later attempt for the same key fails that condition and returns the stored decision. The part people miss is that you have to store the result, not just the fact that you have seen it — otherwise the retry gets 'yes, seen it' and nothing useful."

Follow-up: "Why a cache in front of the durable store, and not just one of them?"

"Different jobs. The counters need sub-millisecond reads and rich structures — sliding windows per card, per device, per merchant — so a cache is the right shape. The durable store is what makes the decision survive and stays authoritative. Cache first, durable store on a miss. And I would want the cache lifetimes written down as a business decision, not chosen by whoever wrote the code."

Follow-up: "How stale can a cached value be?"

"That is a risk question, not a technical one, and it should have an owner. For something that only affects ranking, seconds are fine. For anything that changes whether a payment is approved, I want the number written down, because 'it was cached' is not an answer you can give after an incident."

Follow-up: "Why containers rather than functions on this path?"

"Both have a warm-up cost. The difference is where it lands. A function pays it on a request, every time it needs a new environment — and published guidance puts that anywhere from a hundred milliseconds to a couple of seconds, which for a JVM is at the bad end. A container pays it at deploy and scale-out, off the request path. Inside a hard budget I would rather pay it where the customer is not waiting — and accept that I now have to keep capacity ahead of demand instead of scaling reactively."

Then offer the inversion, before they ask: "If the traffic were spiky and low-volume, or the budget were seconds rather than milliseconds, I would use functions and take the operational saving. The duty cycle and the budget decide it, not a preference."

Follow-up: "How many instances, and show me the arithmetic."

"Concurrency equals throughput times latency. 350 a second at 100 milliseconds is 35 requests in flight. So I am not sizing for 350 of anything — I am sizing for about 35 concurrent, plus headroom. At roughly twenty workers per instance that is two instances at steady state, so I would run at least three, spread across three availability zones — and that third one is there for the failure domain, not for throughput."

Follow-up: "What do you scale on?"

"In-flight requests and latency, with CPU as a backstop. CPU is a lagging indicator when your problem is latency — a service waiting on a slow dependency looks idle. And for anything reading a stream, scale on lag, never CPU: lag is the actual service level."

Follow-up: "What breaks first as you scale out?"

"Connections, before compute. Every instance holds a pool to the feature store and to anything relational, so total connections is pool size times instance count — which means scaling out to fix latency can exhaust the thing downstream. I would cap the pools deliberately and treat downstream connection count as a capacity input, not an afterthought."

This is a rarely-given answer and it lands well.

Follow-up (Java-specific, for the developer): "Anything else on the JVM?"

"Cold start and JVM warm-up get conflated, and they are different. Even a freshly started container has worse tail latency until the code is compiled rather than interpreted. So a new instance should not take production traffic the moment it reports healthy — warm it first, gate the health check on warm rather than alive, and shift traffic gradually. That is true whichever compute you pick, and it is the part that gets missed."

Part 5

Signals, timeouts, and what happens when something is slow

This is the material that wins the backend developer, because it is all mechanism and you have real numbers for it.

Mandatory versus optional — the classification is the design

Signals are not equal, and treating them as equal is what makes a platform fragile. An optional signal improves confidence; a mandatory one is required for a safe decision. That single classification is what lets the platform stay up when a vendor has a bad day.

ControlRoughlyThe reasoning
Per-vendor timeout5–10 ms, sometimes up to 50–80 ms depending on the budgetSet from the top down: start from what you promised, subtract your own work, and what is left is what the call below gets. If the vendor's own latency does not fit in that, the answer is not a longer timeout — it is that the call does not belong on this path.
Retries on external callsZero, in the real-time pathAt this budget a single retry with backoff can consume the whole allowance. "Retries are a reliability tool, not a default." At most one retry, and only for an internal idempotent read with jitter.
Circuit breakerOpens on a rolling window when failures or slow calls cross a threshold; stays open ~60 seconds; then probes with a handful of callsStops you calling a dead dependency repeatedly and turning its outage into yours. Per dependency, never global — one vendor being down must not trip breakers for unrelated calls.
Bulkhead~20 concurrent calls per vendor per instance, zero waitOne blocked dependency must not exhaust the threads and connections everything else shares. "Fail fast, don't queue."
Which signals get calledLow risk: none. Medium: cached only. High: live calls.You do not call every vendor for every transaction. That protects both the budget and the bill, and it is a good detail to volunteer.

Follow-up: "Why not make every signal mandatory, for maximum safety?"

"Because then any one vendor's outage becomes mass declines across the bank. A deliberately small mandatory set with safe fallbacks for everything else is what keeps availability and safety in the same design — making everything mandatory optimises for one of them and destroys the other."

Follow-up: "How do you stop fraudsters exploiting degraded mode?"

This is the question that separates a good answer from a great one, and most people have not thought about it.

"When we are degraded, we get stricter, not more permissive. Thresholds tighten, step-up happens more often, risk-based limits shorten, and we watch for anomaly spikes harder. And every degraded approval is tagged and reviewed separately, so degraded mode is measurable rather than invisible. If you do not do that, you have published a recipe: knock over the signal provider and everything gets approved."

Follow-up: "A vendor is slow and traffic is ten times normal. First five minutes — what do you do?"

"Stabilise before scaling. Freeze deploys. Tighten the edge throttles so we shed load deliberately. Turn on degrade mode for optional signals. Cap vendor concurrency and shorten those timeouts. Then, once the tail is back under control, scale."

"Scaling first would make it worse — if the latency is dependency-driven, more instances just means more simultaneous calls into the thing that is already struggling."

Follow-up: "What happens if the whole fraud service is down?"

"The payment path does not wait to find out. The bounded window expires and a predefined policy applies — step up for medium risk, decline for high risk. That is a risk-appetite decision the business signs off, not one an architect makes alone, because it has a loss rate attached. And the alternative is categorically worse: a fraud outage becoming a payments outage."

Follow-up: "Is a timeout an error?"

"No — and this is the line I would want to land. A timeout is a business decision with a loss rate, not a technical failure. You decided in advance what happens when the window expires. If you did not decide, you delegated it to whoever deployed last."

Part 6

The non-critical path — and the Kafka-or-Kinesis question

This is the second half of the platform and the half people under-prepare. Start by reframing the question, because "which streaming service" is the wrong first question.

Open with this — it reframes and it buys you time

"First thing: fraud has two paths with different physics. The decision is synchronous and sits inside someone else's budget. Everything else is asynchronous. So the stream is not on the decision path — the stream is what keeps the decision path's state current."

figure 4 — the producer publishes a fact. Who reads it, and how fast, is none of the producer's business

So: Kafka, or Kinesis?

The honest answer, and it is a good one

Your resume does not name a broker, and "event-driven" on it describes the design pattern, not a confirmed product. So do not name one as fact. The strong answer is: "I'd choose on the estate rather than on merit — whichever the platform team already runs well." That is not a dodge; it is the right answer, and it protects you from the follow-up about partition counts and consumer rebalancing on a product you have not operated.

Then show you can actually make the choice, from the properties — which is what the table below is for.

Reach for…When you need…Because
A log (Kafka, or a managed Kafka)Several independent consumers of the same facts, ordering within a key, replay, and schema governanceA log keeps the events. That is what lets you add a consumer next year, or replay a year of history against a new model, without touching the producer. For fraud that replay requirement is not a nice-to-have — it is how models get trained and how a regulator's question gets answered.

The one-liner if they push for a single answer

"For this workload I'd want a log rather than a queue or a router, because replay and multiple independent consumers are both real requirements — model retraining needs history, and an investigation needs to reproduce a decision years later. Whether that log is self-run Kafka or the cloud's managed equivalent is an estate decision, and I'd take whichever the platform team runs well."

The design details, if they go deeper

QuestionYour answer
How do you lay out topics?One topic per fact type, owned by whoever produces the fact. Payment submitted, decision made, case events, confirmed-mule list. New consumers subscribe; they do not get their own topic. A topic per consumer is how you end up with two hundred topics and nobody owning any of them.
What is the partition key?The immutable subject — the account. Ordering only exists inside a partition, so you key by the thing whose order you actually need, and two payments on the same account must be evaluated in order. Not the payee, not the channel, not whoever is handling the case — those all change, and you cannot move a record after it is written.
Account, or account owner?Account. Ownership changes. Joint accounts change. The account number does not.
How many partitions?Peak rate divided by what one consumer actually processes, then rounded up hard. "At 700 a second, and one consumer handling a couple of hundred, that is about four — so I would start at twelve, not four, because the count is effectively fixed once chosen and it is also the ceiling on consumers in a group."
Consumer groups?One per application. Case management, analytics and audit each get their own, so a slow consumer in one domain cannot create lag in another.
Exactly-once?"Not across systems. What you get is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which behaves like exactly-once from the outside. The broker's own exactly-once stops at the moment your consumer writes to a store outside its transaction."
Poison messages?Bounded retry, then a dead-letter topic carrying the original offset and the reason — never a silent skip. And an alert, because a dead-letter queue nobody looks at is a slower way of losing data.
Retention and replay?Weeks hot in the stream, then out to object storage for years. The stream is not the data lake. And replay has to be safe: separate deciding from acting, so replaying a decision topic does not re-block anyone's account.
Reference state that is "what is true now"?A compacted topic keyed by the subject — latest value per key. "A table in a log's clothing." Consumers can rebuild their whole view from it.
What do you alert on?Consumer lag, not CPU. Lag is the service level.
Follow-up: "How do you guarantee the event fires when the database write succeeds?"

"An outbox. The service writes its row and writes the event into an outbox table in the same local transaction, and a relay drains that table to the topic. That turns an unreliable dual write into one atomic write plus a retryable publish. The cost is a little latency and one more thing to operate — and in payments that is a trade worth making, because for an irrevocable payment, a broker outage that silently drops the event means losing the evidence that a decision was made."

Follow-up: "What if one key is much hotter than the others?"

"Then that one partition is your throughput, no matter how many others you have. A hot key is a design problem, not a capacity problem. Either the key is wrong, or the expensive work needs to move off the consumer path, or you accept it and size that consumer for the peak. Adding partitions does not help — the hash sends the same key to the same place."

Follow-up: "Three identities show up here. Can you name them?"

Volunteer this one — very few candidates separate these, and collapsing them is a real security flaw.

"There are three and people collapse them: the ingesting service's own platform identity, the stream principal it authenticates to the broker with, and the customer the event is about. Authorisation is per principal on topics and consumer groups. And the customer attribution is stamped at ingest, not taken from the payload — otherwise a compromised producer can write events claiming to be anyone."

Follow-up: "Does the event carry customer data?"

"The event references the customer; it does not carry a copy of them. And card numbers never go into logs or events at all — that is not a preference, it is a scoping decision: if they never enter the event infrastructure, that whole estate stays outside the card-data compliance boundary, which materially reduces what has to be audited."

Follow-up: "What's the residual risk in this design?"

Close on this. Very few candidates close on a measured residual risk, and it is the strongest thirty seconds available to you.

"The decision depends on state that is kept current asynchronously, so there is a lag between something happening and it affecting the next decision. I would measure that lag as an explicit service level, because it is the difference between catching the second fraudulent payment and not."

Part 7

The near-real-time path — the third shape

There were three ingestion shapes, not two, and mentioning the third makes the answer sound complete.

Real time

Synchronous. A payment is waiting. Hard budget, bounded, one round trip.

Near real time

Streaming. Session, device and location patterns that only make sense across a window of events, not a single one.

Batch

Overnight partner files and historical analysis. Model training, deep analytics, reporting.

The near-real-time path is the one worth being able to explain, because it answers a question the fast path cannot. "Two logins from opposite sides of the country in ten minutes" is not visible in a single transaction — it needs something holding state across a window of events. That processing runs off the stream, updates the feature store, and changes what the fast path sees on the customer's next transaction. It never sits in the authorisation path itself.

The sentence that ties the three together

"Three ingestion shapes, one event backbone. The synchronous path decides. The streaming path keeps the decision's inputs current. The batch path improves the rules. And none of the three can slow the other two down, which is the only reason the design holds together."

Follow-up: "Why can't the rules engine just do that itself?"

"Because the rules engine evaluates one transaction, now, against inputs it is handed. Impossible travel is a property of a sequence, not of a transaction — you need something holding windowed state per customer. That is a different kind of processing, and it belongs off the hot path."

Follow-up: "So the fast path is always slightly behind reality?"

"Yes — and I would say that before you asked. That lag is the residual risk of the whole design, and the right response is to measure it rather than pretend it isn't there."

Part 8

Wallets, in depth — the GPAY half of the job description

This is the initiative with your name on it. Two wallet ideas, one architecture point, and one thing almost nobody prepares.

The token replaces the card number

The real card number never reaches the phone or the merchant. The scheme issues a token that works on the rails but is domain restricted — bound to that device or that channel, and worthless anywhere else.

A stolen token is a key to a door that only opens for one lock.

The token outlives the card

Lose the card, replace the card, and the card number changes — but the scheme just updates the mapping. The customer's wallet keeps working and they never re-add it.

That automatic refresh is the entire reason the lifecycle path exists.

figure 5 — green and red are fast and synchronous; yellow is a workflow. Getting that split wrong is what breaks wallet provisioning

The three answers, and why the middle one is an architecture problem

Before a card goes into a wallet, the issuer checks that the person adding it is the cardholder. Three outcomes: approve, step up, decline. Easy to say — but the middle one changes the shape of the system.

  • Approve and decline are synchronous. The scheme is waiting, so these have to resolve fast, from signals you already hold.
  • Step up is not. Sending a code and waiting for a customer to find their phone takes minutes. If you hold the scheme's request open while that happens, wallet provisioning feels broken. So you answer "pending" and complete provisioning on a callback — which means the step-up is a small workflow with its own state and its own timeout, not a service holding a thread.
Your own line, and it is the strongest thing you have here

"Three wallets, three separate identity checks, one issuer-side orchestration model. A single 'authenticated, therefore trusted' flag wasn't good enough — the design had to carry which wallet vouched for this, because the assurance behind each one isn't automatically equivalent, and that distinction has to survive into the token lifecycle decision rather than being flattened at the front door."

Follow-up: "Would you build your own token vault?"

"No, and that is a deliberate decision rather than a default. Running a vault means certification, hardware key management and a permanent compliance burden, for something the scheme already operates at scale. The issuer's job here is the decision and the lifecycle events, not the vault. Being able to say 'don't build' clearly is worth more than designing one."

Follow-up: "What happens after a breach, when a lot of cards are reissued at once?"

Almost nobody prepares this. Volunteer it.

"A mass reissue hits the lifecycle path all at once, on the scheme's compromise-response timetable, not ours. So that path has to absorb a burst, not just a steady trickle of individual card events. That is a capacity requirement you would completely miss if you only designed for the normal case."

Follow-up: "Who can suspend a token?"

"The issuer, if it suspects fraud — or the wallet, if the device is reported lost. The policy lives on the issuer side even though the wallet holds the token. That sentence is the whole relationship."

Follow-up: "How is a network token different from a merchant storing your card?"

"A merchant storing your card holds a real credential — breach them and it is a real card number, and they carry the compliance burden. A network token is domain restricted and carries a one-time cryptogram per transaction. One is breach risk and audit scope; the other is neither."

Follow-up: "Have you integrated VCAS?"

"Not VCAS specifically — my Visa work was tokenisation, disputes and authorisation. But the issuer-side shape is one I know well: score in real time, frictionless when it's low risk, step up when it isn't, and own the fallback when a signal is missing. That is the same decision ladder I designed on the fraud path. Is it replacing an existing 3DS solution or is it greenfield, and is the integration direct or through the processor?"

Answer, pivot, question back. The question back proves you know the terrain better than a claim would.

Part 9

Proving it afterwards — audit and observability

In a bank, "it made the right decision" is only half the job. The other half is proving which decision was made, why, and under which version of the rules — sometimes years later.

RequirementHow the design answers it
Explain a decision years laterEvery decision is an event carrying its own inputs — the rules that fired, the features used, the version in force, whether the platform was degraded at the time, and a correlation identifier. You can replay it, not just read a log line saying what happened.
Prove nobody edited the recordWrite-once storage. Even a compromised administrator cannot retroactively alter history, which is the specific thing an auditor needs to trust — access control alone is a policy promise, not a structural one.
Not slow the payment downAudit events are published after the decision, never as part of it. A slow audit consumer cannot block an authorisation.
Find things across servicesOne correlation identifier, created at the edge, carried through every hop, in structured logs with the same fields everywhere.
Know when you are breachingAlarms on the tail, not the average. Plus degraded-decision rate, vendor health, timeout rate, throttle rate, and consumer lag.
Follow-up: "How would you know the audit pipeline itself had silently stopped?"

A genuinely good question, and the obvious answer is wrong.

"Not by checking whether the consumer is running — it can be running and producing nothing. You compare decision volume on the payment path against audit events landed. A mismatch between 'decisions made' and 'decisions recorded' is the real signal."

Follow-up: "Why two separate pipelines for audit and operations?"

"Different retention, different access, different consumers. Audit needs immutability and years; operations needs low latency and alerting. Merging them means compromising one of the two, and it is usually the audit one that quietly loses."

Follow-up: "What did that actually buy the business?"

"Audit evidence requests went from days to minutes, because teams could self-serve against a standard set of queries instead of raising a request and waiting. And it cost the payment path nothing, because the whole pipeline runs after the decision."

Part 11

Cram sheet for this page

  1. Fraud got a slice of a budget somebody else set. Everything follows from that.
  2. Synchronous where something is waiting; asynchronous for everything else.
  3. mTLS says who is on the connection. OAuth says what the software may do. Neither says whose record this is.
  4. Idempotency on the domain identifier, claimed before the work, and store the result, not just the fact.
  5. The events feed the state that the fast path reads.
  6. Fewer signals means stricter decisions — degraded mode tightens, it does not relax.
  7. A timeout is a business decision with a loss rate, not an error.
  8. Key by the account, because ordering only exists inside a partition.
  9. At-least-once plus idempotent processing. Exactly-once stops at the door.
  10. Three wallets, three identity checks, one orchestration model — and the design carries which wallet vouched.
  11. Don't build a token vault. Do design for the mass-reissue burst.
  12. Concurrency equals throughput times latency: 350 at 100ms is 35 in flight.
And the one thing that undoes all of it

Claiming to have operated what you designed. Every sentence on this page is safe if you keep the two halves apart. "Here's what we built and why" is fact. "Here's how I'd realise it on AWS" is reasoning. Blur them once and the rest of the interview is spent testing you on a platform you have not run.
