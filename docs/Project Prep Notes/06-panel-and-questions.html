The Panel, And The Questions

Principal Tech Lead, a Java Developer and a Backend Developer, and the hiring manager has already said they don't expect hands-on. That means the developers are testing whether they can work with you and whether your judgment holds under their questions, not whether you can write their code. Answer short, answer real, and land every answer on a project.

From The Principal Tech Lead

Q: "Walk me through a system you designed under a hard external constraint."

The NAB fraud platform. Open with the constraint, not the tech: a card authorisation has a scheme-mandated response budget, the issuer answers in a few hundred milliseconds or the transaction times out to stand-in, and fraud got a slice of a budget somebody else set. That fact splits the design: synchronous, hard-bounded decision path, one call, decision plus reason code, timeout resolves to a defined fallback. Asynchronous everything else, every decision emits an event, consumers off the authorisation path. 350 TPS, p99 under 100ms, under 0.1 percent timeouts. Then the cost beat: the decision service's availability became everyone's dependency, and the split means the richest fraud features live in the async path, so the inline feature set is deliberately conservative.

Q: "How do you do design assurance on vendor products?"

"I've lived the vendor side: Mastercard, Visa, Fiserv integrations at NAB, and a commercial data management platform under the Westpac migration capability. Three things I check. One: the vendor's integration contract, because vendors sell capabilities and you buy edge cases. Two: failure modes, because every vendor API eventually times out, rate limits you, or changes schema, and the design has to state what happens on each. Three: the exit, data ownership and extraction, because platform replacement is eventually someone's job, and I've been that someone. Design assurance on a vendor is mostly making the failure states explicit before the contract is signed."

Q: "How would you approach the ITTR replacement?"

Rubric first (latency critical? code ownership? coupling? change rate? data gravity? scaling pattern). Then the refresh discipline: freeze integration contracts so consumers don't churn, prove equivalence with parallel run and reconciliation rather than assertion, cut over in waves with rollback paths, decommission only on evidence. Then anchor it: the Westpac consolidation was the identity-and-reconciliation half of a refresh at 325,000-account scale, and the telecom years were platform upgrades under live production load.

Q: "How do you keep governance from slowing delivery?"

Your governance one-liner: guardrails, not gatekeepers. "Publish reference patterns and golden-path templates, enforce them with CI/CD and policy gates rather than review meetings, keep an interface catalogue, run clinics and ADRs, allow time-boxed exceptions, and measure adoption. Governance that shows up as a checklist in the pipeline doesn't slow anyone down. Governance that shows up as a meeting slows everyone down and gets ignored anyway." If they want an example: the contract-first guidance at CBA, adopted because it made delivery faster, not because a committee mandated it.

Q: "What are the trade-offs in that fraud design, and what would you change?"

Use the four beats: constraint, options, choice, cost. Two honest costs: the consolidated decision point adds a dependency whose availability is everyone's, and the sync/async split means real-time features are limited to what fits in the budget, so the richest signals live slightly stale in the async path. Revisit triggers: if the decision hop's latency became material, or rule sets genuinely diverged by product, then a library per ledger beats a shared call.

From The Java Developer

Q: "What's your Java background, really?"

Straight answer: "Eight-plus years of core Java before architecture: multithreaded, protocol-level server code on Linux, Diameter stacks at Samsung, Alcatel-Lucent and Cox, and I was the SS7/Linux/Java technical lead at Kapsch leading gateway teams through a platform migration. Recent years my Java has been design-level and code-review-level rather than daily commits, and I'd rather tell you that than have you find out." Then flip it to collaboration: "What I bring you is someone who reviews your design and your code with respect for what it takes to write it, and who writes the kind of architecture documents you can actually build from."

Q: "What happened to Java while you were away?"

You have a Java refresher in your notes. The honest shape: Java 8 to current in three moves. Language: records for immutable data, sealed types for closed domain models, pattern matching to replace instanceof chains, var for local noise. Runtime: virtual threads in 21 changing how you think about blocking I/O and thread pools. Ecosystem: Spring Boot is the default assumption, and the migration story (javax to jakarta) matters if they're on an older stack. Do not pretend to recent production experience. "I know what changed and why it matters. I'd ramp fast."

Q: "How do you review code or a design?"

Your Code Review Checklist: a scan, not a memorised defect list. Six passes in severity order: correctness first (does it do what the design says), then failure paths (timeouts, retries, idempotency), then concurrency and state, then security boundaries, then readability and naming, then performance only where the design says it matters. "I review for the failure paths first, because that's where production pain actually lives, and I comment on the design if the code is faithfully implementing a bad design."

Q: "Microservices in Java. Worth it?"

Domain-driven answer: "Bounded contexts first, services second. A microservice boundary that doesn't match a domain boundary just distributes your coupling over the network and makes it worse. I've watched a monolith get carved up well (strangler, one domain at a time, events for cross-domain updates) and the deciding factor was always whether the team structure and the domain model agreed, not the technology."

From The Backend Developer

Q: "Design me a Kafka topic for payment events."

Ten-beat walk, compressed: split the problem first (Kafka is not on the decision path, it keeps the decision path's state current), topic per fact owned by the producer, key by the immutable subject (the account, not the payee or the analyst, because keys can't move after the write), partitions sized by arithmetic (700/sec peak, ~200/sec per consumer, four partitions plus headroom), acks and min.insync.replicas against durability needs, compacted topics for reference state, consumers idempotent because rebalances and retries will hand them duplicates, and the outbox pattern if the producer has a database that must agree with the event.

Q: "How do you actually implement idempotency?"

"Idempotency key from the domain identifier, enforced at the earliest layer that can detect the duplicate, which is usually the edge, with a short-lived store of seen keys. In our fraud decision service the decision store doubled as the idempotency store: a retry got the stored decision, same answer, no second evaluation. The subtle part is choosing the key: it has to be the business event's identity, not the transport's, or a retry over a different path double-processes."

Q: "DynamoDB or RDS, and why?"

Your decision framework, not a preference: "Access pattern first. If I can name every query up front and the load is key-shaped, DynamoDB gives predictable latency at any scale and no connection-pool ceiling. If the access patterns are open-ended, relational, ad-hoc joins, reporting, RDS (or Aurora) is the honest answer. At NAB the decision store was DynamoDB because access was exactly-keyed, and velocity counters lived in Redis because they were derived rolling windows, which is a cache-aside shape."

Q: "What does release readiness mean to you?"

"Rollback, reconciliation, and evidence. Can we reverse or neutralise the change. How do we prove, during parallel run, that the new path agrees with the old, continuously, not at cutover only. And what evidence do we keep: decision logs, audit trails, drift metrics. I designed the coexistence reconciliation half of that at Westpac, and the irreversible operations got approval gates in front of them, because a re-age that reaches a credit bureau cannot be un-sent."

Questions To Ask Them
  • "What's actually in scope for the ITTR refresh: infrastructure, platform and vendor product versions, or application rewrites too? And how do cutovers run, maintenance windows, parallel run, or big bang?" (shows you think about the riskiest part of a refresh first)
  • "Where do design decisions get recorded, and what does design assurance look like here, review boards, ADRs, pipeline gates?" (your governance strength)
  • "How does the team split solution design from delivery, and where does the technical lead sit relative to the platform engineers running OpenShift?" (respects their territory)
  • "For GPAY and VCAS, is the issuer-side integration direct or via the processor, and is VCAS replacing an existing 3DS solution or greenfield?" (proves you know the message-level terrain)
  • "What does success look like for this role at six months?" (closes well every single time)
