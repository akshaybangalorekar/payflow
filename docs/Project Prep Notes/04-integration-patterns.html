Integration Patterns: Most Important / Bare Minimum

Nine integration patterns. This is the page the backend developer effectively interviews from, and half of what the Principal Tech Lead's design assurance questions reduce to.

1. The Secured API Edge

Layered, because no single control survives contact with the internet: WAF at the edge, then API Gateway enforcing mTLS (who is on this connection) and OAuth2 (what is this software allowed to do) as separate questions, per-client throttling, request size limits, strict method allowlist, and object-level authorization (BOLA) checks in the service, because gateway auth answers "which client", never "which of this client's customers".

figure 1 — four layers, four different questions. Being able to say which layer answers which is the whole point
The One Distinction To Hold Onto "mTLS answers 'who is on this connection'. OAuth2 answers 'what is this software allowed to do'. They are different questions, and you need both, and neither of them answers 'which customer's data is this', which is why BOLA checks live in the service."
2. Contract-First, Versioned APIs

The contract is designed and published before implementation; consumers build against it; changes are versioned so consumers migrate on their schedule. This was the core of your CBA CoE guidance and your answer to "how do you handle consumer impact from API changes": protect consumers by contract, never surprise them.

3. Kafka Done Properly (Topic And Partition Design)
  1. One topic per fact type, owned by the producer. New consumers subscribe, they don't get new topics. A topic-per-consumer estate is how you end up with 200 topics and no owner.
  2. Partition key is the immutable subject: the account, not the payee, not the channel, not whichever analyst owns the case. Ordering is only guaranteed inside a partition, so key by the thing whose ordering you actually need.
  3. Partition arithmetic: at a peak of 700 per second and a consumer instance handling a couple of hundred with its processing, you need about four in parallel. Partition count is effectively fixed once chosen, so size for headroom.
  4. Reliability settings: acks and min.insync.replicas chosen against your durability requirement, not by default.

Where you used it: the near-real-time digital fraud path at NAB, plus your Kafka-for-fraud design walk which is the ten-beat spoken version.

5. Batch And File Integration (Still Real In Banks)

Direct Entry files, bureau feeds, start-of-day snapshots. The failure modes are old and unforgiving: a late, missing or duplicated start-of-day file means a stale or non-idempotent view of ledger ownership, and downstream decisions are made on it. Design for arrival detection, duplicate handling, and explicit staleness refusal rather than silent processing. Your CO-04 failure surfaces note is the source.

6. Enterprise Integration Platform (The EIG To EBIP Story)

The strategic frame you can quote from Westpac: the legacy integration capability (ETL, batch, files, transformation) is being replaced by one focused on API services, real-time event integration and orchestration, with an API gateway, security and governance at its core. The reason, verbatim from your notes: "without it, every system builds point-to-point integrations. With it, each system integrates once through a common platform." If this role's Digital initiative touches anything similar, you have watched this transition happen.

7. Identity Translation (Surrogate Keys)

Source identifiers change constantly (card replacement, product transfer, brand migration). A stable surrogate with bidirectional old-to-new mapping, on both batch and real-time paths, is what lets consumers stay ignorant of migrations. Your CO-03 note calls the mapping repository the single highest-value data asset in the estate: lose it and every downstream key becomes meaningless at once, so its recovery point effectively sets the RPO for the whole servicing chain.

8. Multi-Rail Payment Integration

Know the map in one breath: NPP for real-time 24/7 account-to-account (Osko, PayID, PayTo), Direct Entry for batch files, card schemes for merchant payments with online authorisation and batch clearing, RITS for high-value RTGS, SWIFT for cross-border, and BIS Nexus as the emerging cross-border instant connector. Your NAB features sat on both the real-time and batch sides: USL Value Post for posting from partners, PayAll riding Direct Entry.

9. Cross-Trust-Boundary Event Ingress

External partners and trusted internal workloads are structurally different actors and should not share one ingress shape. Your AR-07 decision: partner ingress authenticates and validates at a dedicated boundary with its own failure isolation, while internal workloads get a simpler path, because forcing both through one gate means the partner path's risk controls tax every internal event. Classic bulkhead thinking at the platform level.
