Design Patterns: Most Important / Bare Minimum

Twelve patterns. Five you must be able to defend cold, seven you should recognise and place. Every one is anchored to a place you actually used it, because in this panel "I read about it" dies and "here is where it bit me" wins.

First, The Shape Of Every Answer

Your Decision Inventory note has the four-beat shape. Use it for every pattern question and every design question:

  1. The constraint that forced the decision. Never open with the technology.
  2. The options. At least two, both genuinely viable.
  3. The choice, and the reason in one sentence.
  4. What it cost, and what would make you revisit it. Almost nobody offers beat four. It is the highest-scoring clause available to you.
The Five To Master First

1. Synchronous decision, asynchronous everything else MUST

When a caller is waiting (an authorisation, a servicing request), the path is synchronous, hard-bounded, and does one thing. Everything that doesn't have to answer now is an event. The failure mode it prevents: a reporting consumer slowing down an authorisation.

Where you used it: the entire NAB fraud split. Inline decision API plus Kafka event fan-out to case management, feature store, audit, analytics. None of them on the authorisation path.

Say It "The decision is synchronous because a payment is waiting on it. Everything else is asynchronous because nothing is. Kafka isn't on the decision path. Kafka is what keeps the decision path's state current."

2. Timeout ownership and fallback design MUST

The client owns the timeout, not the server. Every timeout must resolve to a defined fallback, decided in advance. A slow decline and a fast decline look identical to the customer but land very differently on losses and approvals.

Where you used it: the scheme-mandated authorisation response budget at NAB. Fraud got a slice of a budget somebody else set, and the design had to answer "what happens when the slice expires" before anything else.

Say It "The client owns the timeout. You do not hold a customer at a checkout while you think about it. A timeout has to resolve to a defined fallback, because an undefined one is a design decision you've delegated to whichever engineer deploys last."

3. Idempotency on the domain identifier MUST

Retries are a fact of distributed life. Idempotency keys enforced before processing, duplicates short-circuited at the earliest layer that can see them, and the key should be the domain identifier, not a transport artifact.

Where you used it: the NAB fraud decision store: idempotency plus correlation IDs, decisions stored in DynamoDB for quick reuse, enforced so a client retry of the same authorisation gets the same decision instead of a second evaluation.

Say It "Idempotency on the domain identifier, enforced at the layer where the duplicate is cheapest to stop, which in our case was the edge and the decision store."

4. Circuit breaker, bulkhead, graceful degradation MUST

Circuit breakers stop you repeatedly calling a dead dependency. Bulkheads stop one failing dependency from exhausting the threads or connections everything else needs. Graceful degradation decides, in advance, what gets dropped when load exceeds capacity.

Where you used it: mandatory versus optional signal classification at NAB. Optional signals get skipped under load to protect tail latency. Missing mandatory signals trigger a safe fallback (step-up or MFA) rather than blowing out p99. Admission control at the edge returns 429 rather than queueing into oblivion.

Say It (their favourite: what breaks first at 10x traffic) "First things to break are external signals and downstream enrichment services: timeouts plus rate limiting plus queueing equals p99 explosion. I stabilise with admission control at the edge, circuit breakers and bulkheads on dependencies, and the mandatory/optional signal split so degradation is designed, not discovered."

5. Strangler fig plus anti-corruption layer MUST

The only sane way to replace a platform that cannot stop running. Route by route or wave by wave, new capability grows around the old, and an anti-corruption layer keeps the legacy's model from leaking into the new design during coexistence.

Where you used it: this is the Westpac Gemini story told as a pattern: coexistence of interim and target platforms, wave-based cutovers, reconciliation to prove the new path matches the old, and an identity translation layer (surrogate keys) so no consumer ever had to know which side of the migration an account sat on. Also your modernization rubric's answer for a COTS platform like ITTR.

Say It "Strangler for the shape, anti-corruption layer for the years in between, and reconciliation as the proof both sides agree. The pattern is easy. The discipline is proving it daily, which is what the drift-detection design was for."
The Seven You Should Place Confidently

6. Policy decision point versus enforcement point

The PDP decides, the PEP applies. Your flagship version: two card ledgers after an acquisition, each with its own API doing its own authorisation. You consolidated the decision so routing followed the policy outcome. "The thing that has to be consistent is the decision, not the storage."

7. Log versus table (compacted topics)

The stream tells you what happened, the table tells you what is true now. Projections off the log are rebuildable, which is the whole point. Reference state (is this account a confirmed mule) uses a compacted topic keyed by account: latest value per key, a table in a log's clothing. From your Kafka-for-fraud walk.

8. Cache-aside versus read-through

NAB fraud used Redis cache-aside because velocity counters need rich structures and app-managed TTLs across derived keys. A pure single-item exact-key lookup would suit DAX, which is genuinely write-through with no invalidation window. Choosing between them is an access-pattern question, not a preference.

9. Outbox pattern

Service updates its database and publishes an event as one reliable unit, via a table the publisher owns that a relay drains to the topic. The answer to dual-write inconsistency, and the honest answer to "how do you guarantee the event fires" in any interview.

10. Reconciliation and drift detection

Not glamorous, and the difference between a migration that is correct and one that is correct once. Continuous proof during coexistence, not just at cutover. Your MI-10 design made drift a measured, alertable condition between legacy and target platforms.

11. Canonical at the contract

One agreed meaning at integration boundaries and on event topics; autonomous systems behind their own adapters. The test for the anti-pattern: if adding a field for one consumer forces every other consumer to change, you have built the old failed enterprise model.

12. Approval gates on irreversible operations

Every action that cannot be undone (a bureau feed, a re-age, a hard delete) gets a human or automated gate in front of it, Step Functions style, with data quality checks before the point of no return. Your re-age to SRDE to credit bureau risk story is the reason this pattern exists.

If you only revise one thing

Patterns 1, 2 and 4 all answer the same underlying question: what happens when a dependency is slow or dead? If you can walk a panel through degraded-mode behaviour end to end using the NAB fraud platform, you have covered more interview surface than any list of pattern names.

Modernization Rubric (For The ITTR Question)

Your six-question rubric for any "how would you modernise X": Is it latency and availability critical? Do we own the code? How coupled is it (shared DB, ESB dependencies)? Release frequency and change rate? Data gravity and compliance? Expected scaling pattern (spiky, steady, batch, streaming)?

Then match the archetype: a stable legacy monolith is rehost then refactor. A COTS vendor product (ITTR shaped) is replatform plus integrate, avoid deep refactor. An ESB-heavy legacy integration estate refactors to API-led, event-driven, gateway-governed: which is literally the EIG to EBIP transition you can talk about from Westpac.
