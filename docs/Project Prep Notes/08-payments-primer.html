Payments, explained properly

You designed solutions in this space, so the panel will assume you know the basics cold. This page builds the whole domain from nothing — who the players are, what actually moves, and what the words mean — then gives you the flows and the follow-up questions. If you read one page twice, read this one.

How to use this page

Every section ends with drop-down follow-ups. Read the section, then cover the answers and try each question out loud in your own words. You do not need to say it the way it is written here. Simple English is better — a panel trusts a plain answer more than a polished one.

Part 1

Who is actually in the room

A card payment has five parties, and almost every payments question is really asking "which party does this, and why them?" Learn the five and most of the domain falls into place.

PartyWho they areWhat they actually do
CardholderThe customerTaps, dips, or types a card number.
MerchantThe shop, the websiteWants to know, in about a second, whether they will get paid.
AcquirerThe merchant's bankSigns up the merchant, takes the payment request, puts it onto the network. Easy way to remember: the acquirer "acquires" the merchant as a customer.
SchemeVisa, Mastercard, eftposThe network in the middle. It routes the message, sets the rules, sets the deadlines, and settles the money between the banks. It does not hold anyone's money.
IssuerYour bank — the one that gave the customer the cardMakes the decision: approve or decline. This is where you worked. Often uses a processor (a vendor like Fiserv) to run the card platform on its behalf.
Say it like this

"Four-party model: cardholder, merchant, acquirer, issuer, with the scheme as the network between the two banks. The acquirer is the merchant's bank, the issuer is the customer's bank, and the decision is always the issuer's."

figure 1 — five parties, one round trip, one budget that nobody in your bank controls
Follow-up: "Who decides whether a payment is approved?"

The issuer — the customer's own bank. Nobody else can, because only the issuer knows the balance, the limits, the card status and the customer's normal behaviour. The merchant, the acquirer and the scheme all just carry the question and the answer.

Follow-up: "What is a processor, and why does a bank use one?"

A vendor that runs the card platform for the bank — card issuing, authorisation switching, posting to the ledger. Banks use one because building and certifying a card platform against every scheme is enormous, recurring work that is not a competitive advantage. The bank keeps the decisions; the processor runs the machinery. That is the same sentence as wallets and 3DS, which is why it is worth saying.

Follow-up: "Where does the money actually sit during all this?"

Nowhere new. During authorisation no money moves at all — the issuer just puts a hold on the customer's available balance. The money moves later, in settlement, between the two banks, netted through the scheme. That is the next section.

Part 2

Authorise, clear, settle — the three things people confuse

This is the single most common basic question in a payments interview, and the answer is one sentence.

The sentence that frames everything

"Authorisation is a promise. Clearing is the confirmation. Settlement is the money moving." An approved authorisation places a hold — it does not charge the account. The charge lands when clearing matches the hold and posts it.

figure 2 — the authorisation is a conversation; the money is a separate, later, batch process
StateIn plain wordsThe detail that gets you credit
AuthorisedHold placed, no money movedThe merchant is guaranteed nothing until clearing. A hold can expire unclaimed.
Cleared / postedThe charge lands on the accountThis is when the customer's statement actually changes.
ReversedHold released before settlementDifferent from a refund — nothing was ever posted, so there is nothing to give back.
RefundedMoney returned after postingMerchant-initiated. No scheme dispute machinery involved at all.
DisputedCustomer contests a posted chargeNow it enters the dispute stage machine — part 5.

Stand-inThe issuer was unreachable; someone decided for themA timeout is a business decision with a loss rate attached, not an error. Strong line.
Follow-up: "What is the difference between an authorisation and a charge?"

An authorisation is a hold — the bank sets money aside and promises the merchant it is good for it. A charge happens at clearing, when the merchant submits the sale and the issuer posts it to the account. Clearing is what turns the promise into the charge.

Follow-up: "A customer cancelled a hotel booking but still sees the money missing. Why?"

The hotel placed a hold and never cleared it. The hold only drops when it expires, typically a few days depending on scheme rules, unless the merchant sends an explicit reversal. So the customer's available balance is reduced even though they were never charged. Good answer to add: "the fix is the merchant sending a reversal, not the bank doing anything — which is worth knowing because the customer will call the bank."

Follow-up: "Why do petrol stations pre-authorise more than you spend?"

They do not know the amount until you finish pumping, so they authorise an estimate to be sure the money is there, then clear the real amount. The difference sits as a hold until it expires.

Follow-up: "What is interchange?"

A fee the acquirer side pays the issuer side on every transaction, set by the scheme. It is why issuing cards is a business. You do not need more than that sentence.

Follow-up: "What happens if the issuer doesn't respond in time?"

Stand-in processing. The scheme or the processor decides on the issuer's behalf using rules the issuer agreed in advance — usually approve below a limit, decline above it. The architect's point: that means a timeout is not a technical failure, it is a decision you have delegated, with a known loss rate. Which is exactly why your fraud design made every timeout resolve to a defined fallback.

Part 3

Tokenisation — what GPAY actually is

This is your ground. Two ideas carry the whole thing.

Idea 1: the token replaces the card number

The real card number (the PAN) never goes into the phone or the merchant's systems. Instead the scheme issues a token — a different number that works on the payment rails but is domain restricted: bound to that one device or that one channel, and worthless anywhere else.

A stolen token is a key to a door that only opens for one lock.

Idea 2: the token outlives the card

When the card is lost, replaced or reissued, the PAN changes — but the scheme just updates the mapping from token to new PAN. The customer's wallet keeps working and they never re-add the card.

That automatic refresh is the entire reason the notification service exists.

figure 3 — say the message names out loud: Token Request, Approve Provisioning, notification, then first purchase with a cryptogram
The naming trap — worth knowing

The issuer decision step has two names for the same thing, and a message-level interviewer may use either. "Approve Provisioning" is the name at the Visa API layer. "TAR" (Tokenization Authorization Request) is the same decision as it travels on the network and processor rails — it arrives as a standard authorisation message carrying the wallet's ID, the device information, the wallet's own risk score, and how the card was added. One-liner to keep: same decision, two names, API layer versus rails layer.

Token stateWho moves it thereWhy
ActiveSuccessful provisioningUsable for transactions.
SuspendedIssuer (fraud suspicion) or wallet (device reported lost)Temporary and reversible.
DeletedCustomer removes the card, or the issuer kills the tokenPermanent. The card has to be provisioned again to use that wallet.

And the card underneath has its own states

Worth holding separately, because the two sets get confused. A card goes: not activated (issued but never used) → active → then any of renewed (the expiry rolls over), blocked (lost, stolen or compromised), replaced (a new card number is issued), or closed. The one that matters for wallets is "replaced": the card number changes, the token does not, and the mapping is updated behind the scenes so the customer's wallet keeps working without them re-adding the card. That automatic refresh is the entire reason the notification service exists.

Follow-up: "Why bother with tokens at all?"

Three reasons, in order of how much an interviewer cares. One: the real card number never leaves the bank's world, so a breach at a merchant or a phone gets you nothing. Two: it shrinks the compliance burden for everyone downstream, because they are no longer storing card numbers. Three: approvals go up, because each transaction carries a cryptogram proving the genuine device produced it, so the issuer can be more confident.

Follow-up: "What is ID&V?"

Identification and Verification — the check that the person adding the card to the wallet is actually the cardholder. Three outcomes: green (approve now), yellow (step up — send a one-time code or ask them to approve in the banking app), red (decline). The issuer owns that decision. It is exactly the same green/amber/red shape as your fraud decisioning, which is why it was natural work for you.

Follow-up: "What happens when the card is reissued?"

The card number changes; the token does not. The scheme updates the mapping and notifies the issuer. The customer never notices — and that is the whole point of the token layer.

Follow-up: "Who can suspend a token, and why would you?"

The issuer, if it suspects fraud. Or the wallet, if the device is reported lost. The policy lives on the issuer side even though the wallet holds the token — that is the sentence to say.

Follow-up: "What is the difference between a network token and a card stored at a merchant?"

A merchant storing your card ("card on file") is holding a real credential — if they are breached, it is a real card number, and they carry the compliance burden. A network token is domain restricted and carries a one-time cryptogram per transaction. One is breach risk and compliance scope; the other is neither. This is a genuinely good question to be asked and an easy one to win.

Follow-up: "Three wallets — what actually differs?"

Each wallet runs its own identification and verification before the token service will issue a token, and each attests the cardholder differently. So "authenticated, therefore trusted" is not good enough — the issuer side has to carry which wallet vouched for this, because the level of assurance is not automatically the same. Same issuer backbone, three trust boundaries, one orchestration model. That sentence is your NAB design in one line.

Part 4

3-D Secure and VCAS — in plain English

Tokenisation answers "what number moves". 3-D Secure answers a different question: "is the real customer actually here?" It only applies when the card is not physically present — online shopping.

figure 4 — the goal is not "challenge everyone". It is "challenge only the ones that need it"

The old way was to text everyone a code at checkout. Customers hate it and abandon their baskets. The new way is risk-based: the issuer scores the checkout in real time using device, behaviour and transaction signals, and most checkouts pass with nothing visible at all.

VCAS is Visa's version of that issuer-side capability. It is EMV 3DS compliant and risk-based, and two of its APIs are worth naming: a Card Status API that lets the issuer manage card status in real time rather than through portal clicks and overnight files, and a Confirmed Marking API where the issuer reports back whether a transaction turned out Good, Fraud or Undetermined, so the risk models improve.

The number worth quoting

An Australian issuer moving from one-time-code-for-everyone to risk-based authentication saw challenge rates fall 48%, abandonment drop 113 basis points and fraud drop 6 basis points. Fewer challenges and less fraud at the same time — which is the counter-intuitive result that makes the case.

Follow-up: "Explain 3-D Secure to someone non-technical."

"When you buy online, the shop can see the card number but has no way of knowing it is really you holding the card. 3-D Secure is the step where your own bank checks that. If it looks normal for you, they let it through silently. If it looks odd, they ask you to prove it — a code, or a tap in the banking app."

Follow-up: "Why would a bank invest in risk scoring rather than just challenging everyone?"

Because every challenge costs sales. Customers abandon checkouts. So the bank wants the smallest number of challenges that still catches the fraud. Blanket challenging is safe for fraud and terrible for the business — and the business pays for the platform.

Follow-up: "What does 'liability shift' mean?"

If the bank properly authenticated the transaction and it later turns out to be fraud, the loss moves from the merchant to the issuer. So authentication is not just a control, it changes who pays. That is why issuers care about doing it well.

Follow-up (the one they will ask you): "Have you integrated VCAS?"

Answer it straight, then pivot to what you do have. "Not VCAS specifically — my Visa work was tokenisation, disputes and authorisation. But the issuer-side shape is one I know well: score in real time, frictionless when it is low risk, step up when it is not, and own the fallback when a signal is missing. That is the same decision ladder I designed on the fraud path." Then ask them a question back: "Is VCAS replacing an existing 3DS solution or is it greenfield, and is the integration direct or through the processor?" That question proves you know the terrain better than a claim would.

Part 5

Disputes — a workflow, not an API

A dispute is what happens when a customer says "I did not make that charge" or "the goods never arrived." The thing to understand is that it is a stage machine with deadlines and money at every step.

figure 5 — four stages, four deadlines. The deadlines are the design constraint, not the messages
CategoryExamplesHow liability is decided
FraudCard used without the cardholderAllocation — the scheme evaluates it against its own data and assigns liability automatically, in real time. Decisioning-led.
AuthorisationNo authorisation obtained, wrong amount
Processing errorsDuplicate charge, submitted too lateCollaboration — the scheme asks the merchant for evidence. If they do not respond, liability lands on them. Evidence-led.
Consumer disputesGoods never arrived, not as described
The one-sentence version

"Allocation is automated liability assignment. Collaboration is an evidence exchange."

Your genuinely rare credential

When a dispute crosses currencies, the amount recovered rarely equals the amount originally debited, because the exchange rate moved between the transaction and the resolution. Somebody has to decide who absorbs that difference and post it correctly to the general ledger. Most dispute integrations never reach that problem. Yours did. If they ask about disputes, get to this within a minute — it is the part almost nobody else in the market has touched.

Follow-up: "Why isn't a dispute integration just a set of APIs?"

Because the state lives outside your system and moves on a clock you do not control. A case can sit for weeks, each stage has a response deadline, and missing a deadline loses you the case regardless of merit. So the design is a workflow with timers, durable state and alerting on approaching deadlines — not a request and a response.

Follow-up: "What is a chargeback, in one sentence?"

The customer's bank taking the money back from the merchant's bank, on the customer's behalf, because the customer contests the charge.

Follow-up: "Cross-currency dispute, the recovered amount differs. What now?"

"Somebody absorbs the difference, and the design has to say who, in advance, and post it to the right ledger account. It is not a rounding error — at volume it is a real number, and if you do not decide it explicitly your books do not balance." This is your question. Take your time on it.

Your own connection to this

Two of your NAB features sat on two different rails — one on the real-time side and one riding the batch Direct Entry rail. Worth saying, because it shows you have worked both physics rather than just the card path.

Follow-up: "What is PayID?"

A friendly address — a phone number, email or business number — that maps to a real account, so you can pay someone without knowing their BSB and account number. It is a lookup layer, not a rail.

Follow-up: "Can you reverse a real-time payment?"

Usually not, and that is the point. Once an instant payment has settled the money is gone. Recovery goes through a scam or mistaken-payment process, or a new payment back the other way if the recipient agrees. Compare that with cards, where the hold-then-clear model gives you a window. That contrast is a nice thing to notice out loud.

Part 7

Cram sheet

  1. Authorisation is a promise, clearing is the confirmation, settlement is the money moving.
  2. A hold is not a charge. It expires if nobody clears it.
  3. The acquirer is the merchant's bank. The issuer is the customer's bank. The decision is always the issuer's.
  4. The token is domain restricted and outlives the card. The issuer owns the provisioning decision even though the wallet holds the token.
  5. Allocation is automated liability assignment; collaboration is an evidence exchange.
  6. Frictionless when the risk score is low, challenged when it is not. That is all 3DS is.
  7. A timeout is a business decision with a loss rate, not an error.
  8. In wallets and in 3DS alike: the network runs the machinery, the issuer owns the decision.
If you are asked something here you do not know

Say so and reason from the map. "I don't know that specific rule, but structurally it has to sit either with the scheme or with the issuer, and given it affects liability I'd expect it to be a scheme rule." Reasoning out loud from a model you clearly hold beats a guess, every time.
