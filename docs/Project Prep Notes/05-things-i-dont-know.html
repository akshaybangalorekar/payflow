Things I Don't Know, And Why

None of these appeared in your projects, and every one has an honest reason plus a one-line answer. The rule from your own prep: volunteer the boundary before they find it. An owned gap is a credibility asset. A discovered one collapses the interview.

The Two That Will Definitely Come Up

Kubernetes and Red Hat OpenShift

Why it wasn't in my projects: your container workloads were designed on ECS Fargate. The NAB platform's compute story was ECS containers behind API Gateway, and your cloud work is design-level architecture, not cluster operations. OpenShift specifically is an enterprise Red Hat platform, and bank teams that run it have dedicated platform engineers.

What to say: "My container experience is ECS and Fargate, where I designed the workloads but didn't operate the clusters. Kubernetes and OpenShift are on my study list, and the concepts transfer: pods, deployments, services, ingress and RBAC are the same problems Fargate solves with different knobs. OpenShift adds the opinionated layer on top, the built-in CI/CD, image streams and stricter defaults. Give me the vocabulary in context and I'll hold my own in a design review."

Study action before the interview: one evening on the differences (OpenShift routes vs Ingress, image streams, projects vs namespaces, the developer console), plus be able to explain why a bank picks OpenShift (managed, supported, hardened defaults) over vanilla K8s.

Azure

Why: your banking platforms were AWS. Azure never came into scope on your projects.

What to say: "AWS is where my designs live. Azure I know at the concept-mapping level, what the equivalents are, but I haven't delivered on it. If the role is multi-cloud I'd ramp the same way I ramped into banking from telecom, in months, not years."

The Rest, One Card Each

Terraform / IaC

Why: infrastructure-as-code was platform team territory in the environments you worked; your role sat above the pipeline. Say: "I design what the IaC should express, and I review what it produces, but the platform engineers own the code. I can read Terraform and follow a plan, and CloudFormation concepts are in my certification study."

dbt, Databricks, the data platform

Why: your hands-on integration work genuinely stopped at Kafka. Downstream analytics, medallion architectures and transformation tooling belonged to data teams you fed, not to you. Say: "My integration boundary was getting clean, timely events onto the stream. What the data platform did downstream of Kafka was other teams' work, and I won't claim it."

Python

Why: you are a Java and C++ developer by background; the bank platforms you designed were Java ecosystems. Say: "Java is my language, eight-plus years of it before architecture. I read Python fine, I just haven't delivered in it."

React and frontend

Why: solution architecture, not frontend engineering; your exposure was the APIs and BFF patterns behind apps, not the frameworks on top. Say: "I designed the APIs and the delivery model behind the mobile features, not the UI layer, and the JD doesn't ask for it."

Infrastructure-layer refresh (racks, networks, OS, data centres)

Why: the refreshes you led were platform and application level: software platforms, vendor products, gateway migrations. The physical layer below you worked alongside, never owned. Say: "My refresh experience is platform and integration level, not the infrastructure layer. In an ITTR programme I'd rely on the platform engineers and vendors for that layer, and my value is the integration contracts, the cutover safety and the proof of equivalence around it."

VCAS internals

Why: your Visa work was tokenisation, disputes and authorisation; VCAS is the 3DS authentication product, adjacent but not the same. Say: "I haven't integrated VCAS specifically, but I've integrated Visa products end to end, and the issuer-side responsibilities, the APIs, the sandbox-first approach, are a shape I know well. I've been through the developer portal."

Cloud console operations

Why: architect, not operator, by role design. Say (your calibrated line): "On cloud, I'm an architect rather than an operator. I design on AWS, I can defend every service choice and what it costs, and I'd rather be straight about that than blur it."

Formal people management

Why: your leadership has been through influence: guilds, a CoE, and gateway teams at Kapsch. Say: "I've led teams through architecture and through platform migrations, and run capability uplift across a practice. I haven't held formal line management, and if this role grows into that, it's a gap I'd grow into knowingly."

The Rule
Non-Negotiable Never blur "designed and defended" into "built and operated". The moment you claim hands-on depth you don't have, the next follow-up question ends you. The split answer, what I did as fact plus how I'd realise it as design reasoning, is not hedging. It is stronger, and it demonstrates the exact thing this panel is testing: can you map a real problem onto a platform.
