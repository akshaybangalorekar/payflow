Page 9 · practise out loud

The follow-up bank

Around seventy questions a panel actually asks, grouped by who asks them. Every answer here is written in short, plain English on purpose. You are not meant to recite these — you are meant to read the question, close the drop-down, and say your own version out loud. If your version is shorter than mine, that is better, not worse.

How to work through this page

First pass: read the question, say your answer out loud, then open the drop-down and compare. Second pass: only the ones you stumbled on. Mark your three worst and go back to them at the end of the day. Three fixed answers is a good day's work. Twenty half-read ones is not.

Say this, not that — internal names

Your notes carry your old employers' internal system and programme names. Do not say them in this room. They mean nothing to Westpac, they sound like you leak things, and they cost you a sentence explaining them. Say "the collections platform", "the legacy card ledger", "the integration platform", "the credit bureau feed". Scheme and product names are fine and good to use: Visa, Mastercard, eftpos, Apple Pay, Google Pay, Samsung Pay, EMV tokenisation, 3-D Secure, NPP, Osko, PayID, PayTo, Direct Entry, SWIFT.

A · Opening and framing — the first five minutes

These decide how the rest of the hour is read. Get them calm and short.

"Tell us about yourself."

Ninety seconds, three beats, stop.

"Twenty-two years in solution architecture. The first seventeen were carrier-grade telecom platforms — high volume, low latency, availability that was contractual. The last five have been Australian banking: card fraud, tokenisation and disputes at one bank, and most recently a collections platform consolidation at Westpac. What I do is API and integration architecture in payments — I design the solution, I write the design down so a squad can build it, and I defend it in review."

Then stop talking. Do not walk your whole CV. Let them choose the thread.

"Why this role?"

"Two of the four initiatives you listed are things I have designed for. Wallets and card authentication sit on exactly the work I did on the issuing side, and a platform replacement is what I spent the last year on. So it is not a stretch role for me — it is the same problems in a different bank."

Short is better here. Enthusiasm reads as fluff; specificity reads as real.

"What are you doing at the moment?"

Say it plainly. "My Westpac contract finished on the fifteenth of September, so I'm available now." Do not apologise for being between contracts and do not over-explain it. Contract architecture roles end; everybody in the room knows that.

"How hands-on are you?"

This is the question the whole panel is quietly asking, and the hiring manager has already told you they do not expect hands-on. Answer it before they have to dig.

"Hands-on in design and in review — I'll read your code, I'll sit in your pull request, I'll argue about a contract. I'm not going to be the person writing the service. My last production Java was a while ago and I'd rather say that than have you find it out in week three."

Ending on the honest clause is what makes the first clause believable.

"What do you know about ITTR?"

Do not bluff this one. It is an internal programme name and there is no public definition of it. "I've read it as a technology refresh replacement, but I'd rather you tell me the actual scope than have me guess — is it the platform being replaced, the infrastructure underneath it, or both?"

Asking is the correct answer. Guessing an internal acronym and being wrong is a bad first impression; asking is a normal first day.

B · Principal Tech Lead — architecture and trade-offs

This is the person who decides whether you are senior. Every answer here should end with what it cost you — the fourth beat is the one almost nobody offers. see page 3, "the shape of every answer"

"Walk me through a system you designed."

Use the fraud platform, and use the four beats. Constraint first, never technology first.

"The constraint was a response budget the scheme set, which we only got a slice of. So the design question was not 'how do we score fraud well', it was 'how much can we do inside our slice, and what happens when we run out of it'. That forced one shape: a small synchronous decision path, and everything else asynchronous. The decision API did the minimum. Case management, the feature store, audit and analytics all consumed events off the stream, so none of them could ever slow a payment down. The cost was that the analysts' view lags the decision by a few seconds, and we had to be explicit with the business that this was a deliberate trade, not a bug."

"Why not just make it all asynchronous?"

"Because somebody is standing at a terminal. A payment is waiting for an answer, so that part has to be synchronous — there is no version of 'we'll tell you in a minute'. The rule I use is: synchronous only where a human or a transaction is blocked, asynchronous for everything else."

"What breaks first at ten times the traffic?"

Their favourite question. Answer with a specific thing, not "it depends".

"The external signal lookups. They're the slowest dependency and the one I control least. Timeouts plus retries plus queueing is how a p99 explodes. So I'd stabilise in three places: admission control at the edge so we shed load deliberately rather than queue into oblivion, circuit breakers and bulkheads so one slow dependency can't take the threads everything else needs, and a mandatory-versus-optional split on the signals so that under load we drop the optional ones by design instead of discovering which ones drop by accident."

"What happens when a dependency is slow rather than down?"

This is the harder and better version of the last question, and it is worth being ready for.

"Slow is worse than down. Down fails fast and the circuit breaker opens. Slow holds your threads and takes the whole service with it. So every call gets a timeout shorter than the budget above it, and the timeout is owned by the caller, not the server. And a tripped breaker has to resolve to something — a cached value, a safe default, or a step-up — not to an error page."

"How do you decide a timeout value?"

"Top down from the budget, not bottom up from the dependency. I start with what the caller above me has promised, subtract what I need for my own work, and what's left is what I can give the call below. If the dependency's own latency doesn't fit in that, the answer isn't a longer timeout — it's that the call doesn't belong on this path."

"How would you approach the ITTR replacement?"

Ask first, then answer with the rubric.

"First I'd want to know whether we own the code or it's a vendor product, because that changes everything. If it's a vendor product, deep refactoring isn't available to you — it's replatform and integrate. Then: is it on a critical path, how coupled is it — shared database, shared integration bus — how often does it change, where does the data gravity sit, and what does the load actually look like. Those six answers pick the archetype for you. And whichever archetype it is, the migration itself is strangler plus an anti-corruption layer plus continuous reconciliation, because the old thing can't stop running while you replace it."

page 3, modernisation rubric
"You keep saying reconciliation. What does that actually mean in practice?"

"Running both paths and comparing them, every day, not just at cutover. A migration that is correct on the day is not the same as a migration that is correct. So drift between the old and new platform becomes a measured, alertable number with somebody's name on it. That's what let us keep making cutover decisions with evidence instead of confidence."

"How do you migrate without a big-bang cutover?"

"In waves, with both platforms live, and with an identity translation layer in between so no consumer ever has to know which side of the migration an account sits on. The mapping between the old identifier and the new one is the single most valuable thing in the estate — lose it and every downstream key becomes meaningless at once, which is why its recovery target effectively sets the recovery target for everything else."

"What would you do differently on that migration?"

Have a real answer. "Nothing" is a red flag.

"I'd have built the reconciliation and drift reporting first, before any data moved. We built it because we needed it, which meant for the first stretch we were making decisions on assertions rather than measurements. Same work, wrong order."

"Tell me about a design decision you got wrong."

Pick a real one and say what it cost. The pattern is: what I chose, what I missed, what it cost, what I changed. Avoid the fake-humble answer ("I care too much about quality"). A panel can smell it, and a real mistake told plainly is one of the strongest signals available to you.

"What are the trade-offs in that fraud design, and what would you change?"

"Three real costs. One: the analyst view is seconds behind the decision, because we moved it off the hot path — that's a deliberate trade and the business had to agree to it. Two: optional signals dropping under load means the decision quality is a function of load, which you have to be able to see, so it needs a metric. Three: the more we cached to hit the budget, the more we had to reason about how stale the cached value could safely be. If I did it again I'd make 'how degraded is the decision right now' a first-class number on the dashboard, rather than something you infer."

"When would you not use events?"

"When there's exactly one consumer, no need to replay, and somebody is waiting on the answer. Then a queue or a plain call is simpler and I can reason about it. Being able to rule the fancy option out is worth more than knowing the fancy option."

"How do you make a build-versus-buy call?"

"Is it a differentiator? If a customer would never notice us doing it better, we buy. The second question is the one people skip: what does buying cost me in coupling? A vendor product brings its own data model, its own release cycle and its own SLA, and I have to build a boundary layer to keep those out of my domain. That boundary is part of the cost of buying, and it's usually left out of the business case."

"How do you handle a non-functional requirement nobody will commit to?"

"I write down the number I'm designing to and who owns it, and I put it in the design document as an assumption. Then it's either confirmed or corrected, and both outcomes are wins. The thing I won't do is design to an unstated number, because then the argument happens at go-live instead of at design time."

C · Principal Tech Lead — governance, assurance and vendors

The JD calls this "design assurance and architecture governance" and "delivery of vendor products". It is a third of the role. Do not let it be the part you improvise.

"What does design assurance mean to you?"

"Not 'did you follow the rules'. It's 'is this safe to build, and can you defend it to the people who'll have to run it'. So I'm looking for four things in a design: the constraint that forced it, the option that was rejected and why, what happens when each dependency fails, and who is on the hook at three in the morning. If a design can't answer those, it isn't finished, however good the diagram is."

"How do you keep governance from slowing delivery down?"

"By being early and being cheap. A fifteen-minute conversation at the shape stage is worth more than a two-hour review at the end, and it doesn't feel like a gate. The other half is not reviewing everything — risk-tier it. Something irreversible, customer-facing or crossing a trust boundary gets a real review. A change inside one service with a test suite doesn't."

"How do you do design assurance on a vendor product you can't see inside?"

"You can't assure the inside, so you assure the boundary. Four questions: what is their SLA and how does it compare to mine; how do they version and how much notice do I get; what is my behaviour when they are down; and who owns the data and can I get it out. Then I make the boundary layer a first-class piece of my design — it owns the timeouts, the retries, the credential, and the translation between their model and mine. Without it their next release becomes my outage."

"What if the vendor says their SLA is enough?"

"An SLA is a commercial promise, not an engineering control. It tells me what I get compensated for, not what happens to my customer. So I still need to know what my system does in the minutes they're outside it. Usually that conversation is short, because once you ask 'what do we show the customer during those minutes' everyone agrees you need an answer."

"How do you review a design that somebody else wrote?"

"I read it for the decisions, not the diagram. I want to see at least one rejected option. If there's only one option in the document, either the decision was obvious — fine, say so — or it wasn't made, it was assumed. Then I trace one real request end to end and one failure end to end. Most gaps show up on the failure path."

"How do you push back on an architect more senior than you?"

"With the constraint, not the opinion. 'This design needs the downstream service to answer in 80 milliseconds and its p99 is 400. Which of those two numbers do we change?' That's a question about the system, not about who's right, and it's very hard to wave away."

"What do you put in a solution design document?"

"Context and the constraint. The options I considered and why I dropped them. The component and data flow view with the trust boundaries marked. The non-functional targets and who owns each one. The failure modes and what happens in each. And the things I'm assuming, listed, so somebody can correct them. The options and the assumptions are the two sections people leave out, and they're the two that make the document useful six months later."

"What does release readiness mean to you?"

"That we know how we'd undo it. Concretely: is the change backwards compatible for consumers; if it isn't, who's been told and when; what's the rollback and has anyone actually done it; what are the first three metrics we look at after deploy and who's watching them; and what's the one condition that makes us stop. The last one is the one people skip — deciding the abort condition before go-live night is what stops a three-hour argument at two in the morning."

"How do you handle a change that breaks consumers?"

"You don't break them — you version and let them move on their own schedule. The contract is a promise, and the whole value of a contract is that the consumer doesn't have to watch you. If a break is genuinely unavoidable, then it's a named migration with dates, owners and a deprecation window, not a release note."

"How do you work with enterprise architecture?"

"Both directions. They set the target state and the standards; I work out how one initiative actually gets built inside those standards. And when a standard doesn't survive contact with a real constraint, I take that back to them with evidence rather than quietly working around it. A standard nobody can meet stops being a standard the first time someone ignores it."

D · The Java developer

This person is checking one thing: can you talk to developers without pretending? They are not trying to catch you out on syntax — they are testing whether you will be honest about what you don't do. honesty scores higher than recall here

"What's your Java background, really?"

The word "really" means they expect a hedge. Don't give them one.

"About eight years of core Java, on telecom platforms — high throughput, concurrency, the kind of code where a memory leak shows up as a customer outage. That was a while ago now. Since then I've been on the design side, so I read Java and review Java, I don't write it daily. If you want someone who'll out-code your team, that isn't me. If you want someone who'll argue with your team about a design and understand their answer, that is."

"What happened to Java while you were away?"

Know the shape, not the detail. "Records and sealed types for modelling data properly, pattern matching in switch, var, text blocks, the module system — and the big one is virtual threads, which change the economics of blocking I/O so the reactive style isn't the only way to get concurrency at scale. The part I care about as an architect is virtual threads, because 'is this call blocking' used to be an architectural question and now it's much less of one."

If they go deeper than that, say so: "That's about the edge of what I'd claim without having built with it."

"How do you review code, or a design?"

"I read the boundaries first — what comes in, what goes out, what happens when the thing it calls doesn't answer. Then error handling and retries, because that's where most production surprises live. The question I ask most in a review is 'what happens if this is called twice', because in payments it will be."

"Microservices in Java. Worth it?"

"Worth it when different parts need to change or scale independently and you have the delivery maturity to run them. Not worth it as a default. A distributed system converts local calls into network calls and turns a stack trace into a correlation ID. If the team can't do contracts, versioning and tracing well, you've bought the cost without the benefit."

"What's a service you'd have kept as a monolith?"

"Anything where the split would land a transaction boundary across the network. If two things have to be true together, keep them together — the moment you split them you've signed up for sagas, compensations and reconciliation, and you should only do that when something else forces it."

"Spring Boot — how much do you actually use?"

Be exact. "I read it and I design for it. I'm comfortable with the shape — dependency injection, starters, config profiles, actuator for health and metrics. I'm not going to claim I'm current on the framework's internals." Precision about the edge of your knowledge is the point of this whole section.

"How would you debug a latency problem you can't reproduce?"

"Start with where the time goes, not with the code. Distributed tracing across the request to find which hop owns the latency, then look at whether it's all requests or the tail. Tail-only usually means garbage collection, connection pool contention, or a dependency's own tail bleeding into yours. Broad and steady usually means you've added work to the path. Different problems, different fixes — and guessing between them is how a week disappears."

"What makes a good pull request, from your side?"

"Small, and it says what it's for. If I have to reconstruct the intent from the diff, the review is going to be about style instead of about whether it's right."

"Have you managed developers?"

Be honest about the shape. "Technically, yes — I've led teams on design and delivery and been the person they escalate to. Formal people management, performance reviews and hiring, no. I've worked alongside delivery managers who owned that."

"How do you get a team to adopt a pattern they don't want?"

"You don't, by telling them. I'd rather lose the argument and be shown a better answer than win it and have the team route around me. What works is making the cost visible — the incident, the duplicate charge, the consumer that broke — and then the pattern is obviously the cheap option rather than my preference."

E · The backend developer

Concrete, mechanism-level questions. Short answers with a real example beat long answers. page 4 is the source for most of this

"Design me a topic for payment events."

"One topic per fact type, owned by whoever produces the fact — not one topic per consumer, because that's how you end up with two hundred topics and no owner. New consumers subscribe to the existing topic; they don't get their own."

"The partition key is the thing whose order I actually need, and it has to be immutable — the account, typically. Not the payee, not the channel, not whoever's handling the case, because those change. Ordering is only guaranteed inside a partition, so keying wrong means your ordering guarantee is a coincidence."

"Partition count I'd size for headroom, because it's effectively fixed once chosen — peak rate divided by what one consumer instance can actually process, then round up generously."

"How many partitions, and show me the arithmetic."

Do it out loud, with the units. "If peak is around 700 events a second, and one consumer instance handles a couple of hundred a second with the processing it has to do, that's 700 divided by 200, so four in parallel, and I'd size above that for headroom and for the fact that you can add partitions but not easily remove them. And I'd say the assumption out loud: that 200 is the number I'd want to measure, not assume."

Saying which number you'd verify is the senior move.

"What if one key is much hotter than the others?"

"Then that partition is your throughput, no matter how many others you have. A hot key is a design problem, not a capacity problem. Either the key is wrong, or the work on that key needs to move off the consumer path, or you accept it and size that one partition's consumer for the peak."

"How do you actually implement idempotency?"

"Key on the domain identifier, not on something the transport made up. A message ID changes when somebody retries through a different path; the payment identifier doesn't. Then check-and-claim in one atomic operation at the earliest layer that can see the duplicate, and if it's a repeat, return the original result rather than doing the work again."

"The part people miss: you have to store the result, not just the fact that you saw it. Otherwise the second caller gets 'yes, seen it' and nothing useful."

"Exactly-once — is it real?"

"Not end to end across systems. What you get is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing, which behaves like exactly-once from the outside. Anyone selling you exactly-once across a network boundary is describing something narrower than it sounds."

"How do you guarantee the event fires when the database write succeeds?"

"The outbox pattern. The service writes its row and writes the event to an outbox table in the same local transaction, and a relay drains that table to the topic. That turns an unreliable dual write into one atomic write plus a retryable publish. The cost is a little latency and a component to operate."

"What's a poison message and what do you do with it?"

"A message that fails every time, so it blocks the partition behind it while you retry forever. You need a bounded retry and then somewhere to put it — a dead letter topic with enough context to work out what happened, and an alert, because a dead letter queue nobody looks at is just a slower way of losing data."

"SQL or NoSQL for this, and why?"

"Access pattern first, not preference. If I know the key and I need single-digit millisecond reads at high volume, a key-value store fits and I accept that I'm designing for one access pattern. If I need to ask questions I haven't thought of yet, or I need a transaction across rows, relational, and I accept it scales differently. The wrong version of this answer is picking the technology first and then justifying it."

"Cache-aside or read-through?"

"Cache-aside when the application needs to control what's in there and for how long — derived values, counters, several keys with different lifetimes. Read-through when it's a straight single-item lookup by exact key and you'd rather not manage the invalidation window. It's an access-pattern question."

"How stale can a cached value be?"

This is the question behind the question, so have the answer. "That's a business decision and it should be written down. For something that only affects ranking, seconds are fine. For anything that affects whether a payment is approved, I want to know the number and who owns it — because 'it's cached' is not an answer you can give after an incident."

"What's the difference between a queue and a log?"

"A queue hands a message to one consumer and it's gone. A log keeps it, so several independent consumers can read the same facts at their own pace, and you can replay. That's the whole reason to reach for Kafka rather than a queue — if you don't need replay or multiple consumers, you probably don't need Kafka."

"When would you use a compacted topic?"

"When I want the latest value per key rather than the history — reference state, like whether an account is flagged. It's a table wearing a log's clothes. Consumers can rebuild their whole view from it without me having to keep every event ever published."

"How do you secure an API that a partner calls?"

"Layers, because no single control survives the internet. Edge filtering, then the gateway doing two different jobs: mTLS answers 'who is on this connection' and OAuth answers 'what is this software allowed to do'. Throttling and size limits per client. And then the bit that lives in the service, not the gateway: object-level authorisation — the gateway can tell me which client is calling, but it can never tell me whether this customer's record belongs to that client. That check has to be in the service, on every read."

"Give me an example of that object-level check going wrong."

"The classic one is an endpoint that takes an identifier in the path, authenticates the caller properly, and then trusts the identifier. The caller is real; the record isn't theirs. It passes every gateway control and every penetration test that only checks authentication. The fix is boring: the query is always scoped by the caller's own context, never by the identifier alone."

Worth rehearsing this one until it is automatic — it is the single most common serious API flaw, and it is a good answer to give before you are asked.

"How do services authenticate to each other?"

"With their own identity, short-lived, issued by the platform — not a shared secret in a config file. And the important point is that the user's identity and the service's identity are two different things. A service being allowed to call an API does not mean it's allowed to act for that customer. Keeping those separate is what stops one compromised service reading everything."

"Batch files still exist. What goes wrong with them?"

"Three things, and they're old and unforgiving. The file is late, the file is missing, or the file arrives twice. Each one produces a stale or double-counted view that downstream decisions are then made on. So the design needs arrival detection with an expected-by time, duplicate detection on file identity, and an explicit refusal to process stale data rather than quietly processing it. Silently using yesterday's file is the worst of the four outcomes and the easiest one to build by accident."

F · Payments quick-fire

They will assume you know these cold, because your CV says payments. One or two sentences each. If you need more than that, you are explaining rather than answering. full explanations on page 8

"Authorisation versus clearing versus settlement."

"Authorisation is a promise — a hold, no money moves. Clearing is the confirmation, and that's when the charge actually posts. Settlement is the money moving between the banks. Different systems, different timescales."

"Who is the acquirer and who is the issuer?"

"The acquirer is the merchant's bank — it acquires the merchant. The issuer is the customer's bank — it issued the card. The decision is always the issuer's."

"What is a token and why does it matter?"

"A number that works on the payment rails but is restricted to one device or one channel, so the real card number never reaches the phone or the merchant. And it outlives the card — reissue the card and the mapping updates, so the customer's wallet keeps working."

"Walk me through adding a card to a wallet."

"The customer adds the card; the wallet asks the scheme's token service for a token; the token service asks the issuer to approve it; the issuer decides — approve, decline, or step the customer up; the token is created and pushed to the device, and the issuer is notified so its own registry stays in sync. First purchase then carries the token plus a one-time cryptogram."

"What is ID&V?"

"The check that the person adding the card is the cardholder. Three outcomes: approve, step up, decline. The issuer owns that decision even though the wallet is running the flow."

"What is 3-D Secure, in one sentence?"

"The step where the customer's own bank checks it's really them, on an online purchase where the card isn't physically present. Low risk goes through invisibly; high risk gets challenged."

"Have you integrated VCAS?"

Straight answer, then pivot, then a question back. "Not VCAS specifically — my Visa work was tokenisation, disputes and authorisation. But the issuer-side shape is one I know well: score in real time, frictionless when it's low risk, step up when it isn't, and own the fallback when a signal is missing. Is it replacing an existing 3DS solution or is it greenfield, and is the integration direct or through the processor?"

"What is a chargeback?"

"The customer's bank taking money back from the merchant's bank on the customer's behalf, because the customer contests the charge. And it's a staged workflow with a deadline at every step, not an API call."

"Why is a dispute integration hard?"

"Because the state lives outside your system and moves on somebody else's clock. Miss a response deadline and you lose the case regardless of merit, so it's a durable workflow with timers and alerting, not a request and a response."

"What happens if the issuer doesn't respond in time?"

"Stand-in — the scheme or processor decides on the bank's behalf using pre-agreed rules. Which means a timeout isn't a technical failure, it's a decision you've delegated, with a known loss rate."

That last sentence is one of your strongest lines. It connects payments knowledge to architecture thinking in one move.

G · Behavioural

Short story, real detail, and say what you did rather than what the team did. Two minutes maximum.

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder."

Structure: the disagreement, what you did about it, the outcome, what you'd do differently. The best version ends with you having changed your position on something, because that is the part that proves you were actually listening rather than waiting.

"Tell me about a time you worked with ambiguity."

Use a real one. The shape that scores: "I couldn't get the requirement, so I wrote down the assumption I was designing to, put my name on it, and circulated it." Then either it got confirmed or it got corrected, and both moved the work forward. Waiting for clarity is the answer that doesn't score.

"Tell me about a delivery that went badly."

Pick something real and own your part of it. Do not pick a story where every problem was someone else's — that is the most common way this question is failed. What they're listening for is whether you can describe a failure without becoming defensive.

"How do you handle a deadline you don't think is achievable?"

"I say so early, with what's driving it, and I bring options rather than just the problem — what we'd have to cut, what we'd have to accept, or what we'd have to add. Saying it late is the only genuinely unprofessional option."

"What keeps you up at night on a payments platform?"

"Silent wrongness. An outage is loud and everybody responds. A system that's quietly making slightly wrong decisions — a stale reference file, a degraded signal, a duplicate that got posted — runs for days before anyone notices, and by then you have a reconciliation problem instead of an incident. Which is why I'm insistent about drift detection and about making degradation a visible number."

"Why contracting rather than permanent?"

Answer it plainly and without defensiveness, then say what you want next. If you'd take a permanent role, say so — this question is usually asked because they want to know whether you'll stay.

"What do you want to learn in this role?"

Name a real gap, which makes you credible rather than needy. The container platform side is the honest answer — you design for it and you don't operate it, and a role with OpenShift in it is where that closes. Do not name something you can already do.

"Where do you see yourself in a few years?"

Keep it in the same direction as the role. Something like: "doing this, at a bigger scope — owning the design across a domain rather than an initiative." Avoid answers that imply you want their boss's job or that you'd rather be doing something else.

H · Questions to ask them

Ask three, not eight. The good ones are the ones only somebody who has done the job would think to ask.

  • "What's actually in scope for the ITTR replacement — the platform, the infrastructure underneath it, or both?"
  • "Is the VCAS work replacing an existing 3DS solution, and is the integration direct or through the processor?"
  • "Who signs off a design here, and what does that forum actually look like?"
  • "How much of the estate is vendor product versus built in-house? That changes what solution design means day to day."
  • "What's the thing you'd most want fixed in the first three months?"
  • "Are the squads shaped around systems or around customer journeys?"
Closing line, if you get the chance

"Two of these four initiatives are things I've designed for on the issuing side, and the third is a platform replacement, which is what I spent last year on. The container platform side is the part I'd be learning, and I'd rather say that now than discover it with you in week three."

If you are asked something you do not know

Say so, then reason from the model. "I don't know that specific one, but structurally it has to sit either with the scheme or with the issuer, and given it affects liability I'd expect it to be a scheme rule." Reasoning out loud from a model you clearly hold beats a guess, every single time — and this panel will be testing for it deliberately, because the hiring manager has already told them you are not being hired for hands-on recall.
