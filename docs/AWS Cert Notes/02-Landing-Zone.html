The AWS Landing Zone

Accounts, networks and guardrails — the layer that exists before any of your services do. Book 01 answers "which service, and why". This book answers the question that comes first in every real engagement: where does it all live, who is allowed in, and what stops a mistake in one place from reaching another?

SECTION 1The account is the wall

↑ contents

Start with the thing that makes AWS estates look the way they do.

Inside a single AWS account you have plenty of ways to separate things — security groups, IAM policies, separate VPCs, tags. All of them are walls inside one building. The account itself is a separate building. It is the only boundary where a mistake on one side genuinely cannot reach the other, and that difference is why large organisations end up with dozens or hundreds of accounts rather than one.

The five reasons, in the order they actually matter

1. Blast radius. If one workload is compromised, or somebody runs a destructive command in the wrong window, the damage stops at the account edge. In one shared account, an over-permissioned role in a test workload can reach production data, because IAM is evaluating everything in the same place.

2. Limits are per account. Service quotas — how many Lambda functions may run at once, how many network interfaces a subnet may hold, how many requests per second an API may take — are counted per account and per Region. Share an account, and a load test in one team's workload can exhaust the concurrency another team's payment service needs. Separate accounts make that impossible rather than unlikely.

3. Billing separates itself. Cost is naturally attributed per account with no tagging discipline required. Anyone who has tried to answer "what does the fraud platform cost us?" from a single account full of untagged resources will recognise why this matters.

4. Permissions become simple. "You are an administrator in the development account and read-only in production" is a sentence anybody can verify. The single-account equivalent is several hundred lines of IAM conditions on tags, and nobody can tell you with confidence what it permits.

5. The hard ceiling only works at this level. A service control policy — the only mechanism that can say "nobody in here, not even an administrator, may do this" — attaches to an account or a group of accounts. You cannot apply one to half an account. If you need a hard stop, you need an account boundary to hang it on.

BoundaryStops whatHonest strength
Security groupNetwork traffic to a resourceGood, but it is one misconfiguration away from open, and it says nothing about API calls.
IAM policyAPI calls by one identityStrong when correct. Complex to verify, and one wildcard undoes it.
Separate VPCNetwork reachabilityNetwork only. Both VPCs still share one IAM, one set of quotas, one bill.
Separate accountNetwork, IAM, quotas, billing, and the blast radius of a compromiseThe real boundary. Everything above is a wall inside one building.

The argument against, which you must be able to make

More accounts is more to run. Every new account needs network connectivity, logging configured, roles created for your pipelines, guardrails applied and someone paying attention to it. Done by hand, twelve accounts is a part-time job and sixty is a full-time one.

That is not a reason to use one account. It is the reason Control Tower and account automation exist — you make creating a correct account a repeatable, minutes-long operation, and then the count stops mattering. A candidate who says "multi-account, obviously" without acknowledging the operational cost sounds like they have read about it. A candidate who names the cost and then names the automation sounds like they have done it.

Say this out loud. "The account is the strongest isolation boundary AWS gives me — it separates blast radius, service quotas, billing and IAM in one move. Everything smaller is a wall inside the same building. The cost is that accounts have to be created and governed automatically, which is why the landing zone is a platform deliverable rather than a diagram."

SECTION 2The account structure

↑ contents

The three words you need

AWS Organizations is the service that groups accounts together. One account is nominated as the management account — it creates the organisation and it is where billing rolls up. Accounts can be arranged into organisational units, usually shortened to OUs, which are simply folders. Policies attach to the root, to an OU, or to an individual account, and they flow downward.

figure 1 — a structure that survives a reorganisation; AWS's own recommended shape, with a bank's environments added

The Security OU with its Log Archive and Audit accounts is not an invention — it is what AWS Control Tower creates for you, and it is worth knowing by name because interviewers use it as shorthand for "has this person seen a real landing zone".

The one rule that stops the structure rotting

Organise OUs by the policy you want to apply, not by the organisation chart. Teams get reorganised, business units get renamed, and departments merge — roughly every eighteen months in a large bank. Guardrails do not change nearly that often. If your OUs are named after departments, every reorganisation becomes an account-migration project. If they are named after the rules that apply to them — production, non-production, sandbox, regulated data — the structure outlives the org chart.

Why nothing runs in the management account

This one is worth committing to memory, because it is a standard interview question and the reason is specific rather than a matter of taste.

Service control policies do not apply to the management account. They restrict the member accounts only. So anything running in the management account sits above your hard ceiling: your strongest preventive control does not protect it. Combine that with the fact that the management account can create accounts, invite accounts and change the organisation itself, and it becomes the single most valuable target in the estate.

So it holds the organisation and the billing, and nothing else. Access is restricted to a handful of people, with multi-factor authentication, and the root user's credentials go in a safe.

Say this out loud. "No workloads in the management account, because SCPs don't apply to it — anything running there sits outside the guardrails, and it is also the account that can modify the organisation itself."

How many accounts, and what earns one

There is no formula, but there is a usable test: a workload earns its own account when it needs a different guardrail, a different blast radius, or a different bill. In practice that produces one account per application per environment in most banks, which sounds like a lot until you remember the alternative is one IAM policy that nobody can read.

What does not earn an account: a new microservice inside an existing application, a new team working on the same product, or a temporary project. Those are namespaces, not buildings.

SECTION 3Guardrails — what stops people doing damage

↑ contents

There are four mechanisms that get talked about as though they are the same thing, and they are not. Two of them can stop an action before it happens. One of them limits what someone else may grant. One of them only tells you afterwards. Confusing them is one of the most common ways to fail a security-flavoured architecture interview.

          │  │  │  that GRANTS anything.              │  │  │

figure 2 — an action is permitted only if every layer allows it; Config is not a layer

In words: an API call succeeds only if it is allowed by the identity's own policy, and not denied by the SCP, and within the permission boundary if one applies, and allowed by any policy on the resource itself. A single deny anywhere wins.

What a service control policy actually does

Four facts, all of which are worth stating precisely because interviewers probe them.

  • An SCP never grants anything. It sets the maximum. If an SCP allows an action and no IAM policy grants it, the action still fails.
  • SCPs do not apply to the management account. Covered in Section 2, and the reason nothing runs there.
  • SCPs do apply to the root user of a member account. This is what makes them genuinely a ceiling — there is no administrator who can simply switch them off from inside.
  • SCPs do not restrict service-linked roles — the roles AWS services create to work on your behalf. Worth knowing, because it is a common source of "why didn't my deny work?".

The guardrails a regulated organisation usually starts with

GuardrailWhat it preventsWhy a bank wants it
Deny leaving the organisationAn account detaching itself from central governanceWithout it, every other guardrail can be escaped in one API call.
Deny disabling CloudTrail, Config or GuardDutyTurning off the audit trailThe first thing an attacker does, and the thing an auditor checks first.
Deny all Regions except the approved onesDeploying outside the permitted geographyData residency is a legal requirement in Australian financial services — and it shrinks the attack surface at the same time.
Deny creating IAM users and access keysLong-lived credentials appearing in codeForces roles and short-lived credentials. Closes the single most common breach path.
Deny deleting or altering log bucketsEvidence being destroyedThe Log Archive account exists to be write-once; this is what makes that true.

The distinction that gets tested. A service control policy is preventive — the API call fails. AWS Config is detective — the resource gets created and Config tells you afterwards that it should not have been. Both are useful and they answer different questions. A candidate who offers Config as the answer to "how would you stop that?" has just said "I would find out about it later".

Where permission boundaries fit

This one is narrower and often misunderstood. A permission boundary is a ceiling you attach to a single IAM role. Its purpose is delegation: you want a development team to create their own roles for their own applications, without being able to create a role more powerful than they are. You give their role permission to create roles, but only roles that carry the boundary. The boundary then caps everything they create.

If you are not delegating role creation, you do not need one. Reaching for a permission boundary when the question was about account-level restriction is a sign of half-learned material.

Say this out loud. "SCPs are the preventive ceiling and they only ever subtract. IAM policies are the only thing that grants. Permission boundaries cap what a delegated team can create. Config is detective — it reports after the fact, so I would never offer it as a control that stops something."

SECTION 4Who gets in — people and workloads

↑ contents

Two populations, two mechanisms, and they should never be mixed.

People

Humans sign in through IAM Identity Center, which federates from whatever directory the organisation already runs — Microsoft Entra ID in most Australian enterprises, sometimes Okta. The user authenticates once against the corporate directory, then selects an account and a role, and receives temporary credentials that expire.

What this buys you: joiners, movers and leavers are handled by the existing HR-driven identity process rather than by someone remembering to delete an AWS user. Multi-factor authentication is already enforced centrally. And there is no long-lived credential anywhere in the chain.

Permission sets are the reusable definitions — "read-only", "database operator", "network administrator" — which you then assign to a group of people in a set of accounts. One definition, many accounts.

Workloads

Software never gets a user. It gets an IAM role, and the credentials arrive automatically and rotate automatically. An ECS task, a Lambda function and an EC2 instance each have a supported way to receive a role, and none of them require a secret in a configuration file.

The rule with no exceptions. No long-lived access keys in an application, ever. An access key is a password that does not expire, and the overwhelming majority of publicised cloud breaches begin with one that ended up in a repository, a log file, or a laptop backup. If you take one sentence from this section, take this one.

Reaching into another account

A deployment pipeline in the shared services account needs to deploy into the payments production account. It does not get credentials for that account. Instead:

1
TARGET ACCOUNT

A role is created in the payments account with the permissions the pipeline needs — and nothing more.

2
THAT ROLE'S TRUST POLICY

The role says who is allowed to assume it: specifically, the pipeline's role in the shared services account. This is the part people forget — a role has two policies. One says what it can do; the other says who may become it.

3
CALLING ACCOUNT

The pipeline calls sts:AssumeRole and receives temporary credentials, valid for a set period, for the target account.

4
CLOUDTRAIL, BOTH ACCOUNTS

The assumption and every subsequent call are recorded, with the original identity preserved. That trail is the audit evidence, and it is the reason this pattern is acceptable to a risk function where a shared credential would not be.

When the other party is a third party rather than another of your own accounts, the trust policy should also require an external ID — a shared value the third party supplies on every assume call. It exists specifically to prevent the confused deputy problem, where a vendor who holds many customers' role ARNs could be tricked into acting against the wrong one.

Say this out loud. "People federate through Identity Center from the corporate directory and get temporary credentials. Workloads get IAM roles — never keys. Cross-account access is role assumption with an explicit trust policy, and an external ID when the caller is a third party."

SECTION 5Inside one VPC

↑ contents

A VPC is your own private network inside AWS. You choose its address range, you cut it into subnets, and you decide what each subnet can reach. Everything in this section is the part most people half-know, and the half they are missing is usually the route table.

Address planning — the decision that is hardest to undo

A VPC gets a CIDR block, which is just a range of IP addresses written as 10.20.0.0/16. The number after the slash says how many addresses: a /16 gives you about 65,000, a /24 gives you 256.

Two ranges that overlap can never be connected. If your payments VPC and the data centre both use 10.0.0.0/16, no amount of routing will let them talk, because a packet for 10.0.3.7 is ambiguous. The only fixes are re-addressing an entire environment or translating addresses in between, and both are projects rather than changes. This is the single most expensive landing-zone mistake, and it is normally made in week one by someone who did not know there would eventually be forty VPCs and an acquisition.

So: allocate ranges centrally from one plan, for the whole estate, including the data centre and room for acquisitions. AWS has a service for exactly this — VPC IP Address Manager, usually called IPAM — which hands out non-overlapping ranges and tracks what is used.

On sizing, the useful advice is unfashionable: be generous. A /16 per VPC and a /20 or /21 per subnet costs nothing. Addresses are free; running out is a migration. The instinct to be efficient with address space is inherited from a world where it was scarce, and it is not scarce here.

figure 3 — three tiers across three availability zones; the tier is defined by the route table, not by a setting

The misconception worth correcting now. There is no "public" checkbox on a subnet. A subnet is public because its route table has a route to the internet gateway, and private because it does not. That is the whole difference. If you understand route tables, VPC networking stops being mysterious; if you do not, it never stops being mysterious.

Three tiers rather than two is a deliberate choice. The isolated tier has no route to the internet at all — not even outbound through NAT. Your database lives there. It means a compromised database host cannot call out to an attacker's server, which is a control you can point at in a security review.

The NAT gateway, and why it appears on the bill

Resources in a private subnet often still need to reach out — to download a package, to call a partner API, to reach an AWS service. A NAT gateway sits in a public subnet and makes that possible in one direction only: your workload can start a conversation outward, and nothing on the internet can start one inward.

Two things about it that matter architecturally:

  • One per availability zone, for resilience. A NAT gateway lives in a single zone. If you put one in zone A and zone B's workloads route through it, then losing zone A takes out zone B's outbound traffic too — you have quietly created a dependency between two things that were supposed to be independent.
  • It is charged per hour and per gigabyte processed. On estates with chatty workloads, NAT data processing is frequently one of the larger surprises on an AWS bill. Most of it is usually traffic to AWS services that should never have gone through NAT at all — which is the next section.

Security groups and network ACLs

 Security groupNetwork ACL
Attaches toThe resource (an instance, a task, a load balancer)The subnet

The strongest single habit here: reference security groups rather than IP ranges. Saying "the database accepts connections from the application tier's security group" stays correct when the application scales, moves zone or gets replaced. Saying "the database accepts 10.20.16.0/20" is correct until somebody adds a subnet.

Say this out loud. "Three subnet tiers — public for load balancers and NAT, private for compute, isolated for data with no outbound route at all. A subnet is public because of its route table, not a setting. NAT gateway per availability zone so I don't couple two zones together, and security groups referencing each other rather than CIDR ranges."

SECTION 6Reaching AWS services without touching the internet

↑ contents

Here is something that surprises people. S3, DynamoDB, Secrets Manager, KMS and most other AWS services have public endpoints. When your application in a private subnet writes to S3, by default that request goes out through the NAT gateway, onto the public internet, and back into AWS. It is encrypted, and it is authenticated, and it still left your network — and you paid the NAT gateway for every gigabyte of it.

VPC endpoints fix that. They give you a private path from your VPC to an AWS service. There are two kinds and the difference is worth knowing precisely.

 Gateway endpointInterface endpoint
Works forS3 and DynamoDB only. Those two, nothing else.Almost every other AWS service, and third-party services published through PrivateLink.
How it worksAdds a route to your route table pointing at the service. No IP address of its own.Creates a network interface with a private IP address inside your subnet, and DNS resolves the service name to it.
CostFree. No hourly charge, no data charge.Charged per hour, per availability zone, plus per gigabyte.
Reachable from on-premises?No — it is a route table entry, and routes do not extend over a VPN or Direct Connect.Yes. It has a real private IP address, so anything that can reach your VPC can reach it.
Built on PrivateLink?No.Yes.

The practical reading of that table: put gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB in every VPC, always, because they are free and they remove traffic from your NAT bill. Add interface endpoints deliberately, for the services you actually use, because they cost money per zone and a long list of them adds up.

The control nobody uses, and should

A VPC endpoint can carry an endpoint policy. That policy decides which resources are reachable through this endpoint. So you can say: traffic leaving this VPC through the S3 endpoint may only reach our own buckets.

That is a genuine data-exfiltration control, and it is one of the better answers available to the question "how would you stop someone copying data to a bucket they control?". IAM policy alone answers who may call S3; the endpoint policy answers which S3 they may reach from inside your network.

Say this out loud. "Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB in every VPC — they're free and they take that traffic off the NAT gateway. Interface endpoints for the services we actually use, sized per availability zone because that's how they're charged. And an endpoint policy restricting S3 to our own buckets, which is a real exfiltration control rather than just plumbing."

SECTION 7Connecting VPCs to each other

↑ contents

This sounds like one question with one answer, and it is four questions. The thing that separates them is whether you want to join two networks together, or expose one service to a consumer.

OptionWhat it doesChoose it whenThe catch
VPC peering Joins two VPCs so their private addresses can reach each other. You have two VPCs and expect to keep having two. No transitive routing. If A peers with B and B peers with C, A still cannot reach C. Ten VPCs fully connected needs forty-five peerings, and forty-five sets of route table entries.
Transit Gateway A hub. Every VPC attaches once, and the hub routes between them. More than a handful of VPCs, or you want central control of who may reach whom. Charged per attachment per hour and per gigabyte. It is also a thing that can fail, so it belongs in your resilience thinking.
PrivateLink Exposes one service to consumers, one-way, without joining the networks at all. A consumer needs your service but has no business reaching the rest of your network — another business unit, a partner, a SaaS vendor. One-way by design, and per-endpoint cost. Overlapping address ranges do not matter, which is sometimes the entire reason to choose it.
VPC Lattice Service-to-service connectivity with policy, at the application layer, across accounts and VPCs. You want service-level access control without building a service mesh or thinking about routes. Newer, and a different model from route-based networking — so it is an architectural commitment, not a drop-in.

The point that makes this sound senior

Peering and Transit Gateway join networks — afterwards, two things that were separate can route to each other, and the separation is gone. PrivateLink exposes a service — afterwards, a consumer can call one endpoint and nothing else, and the networks remain strangers.

In a bank, that difference is usually a risk decision rather than a networking one. When a partner or a different business unit needs access, "we joined our networks" and "we published one service endpoint" are very different sentences in a security review.

SECTION 8Reaching the data centre

↑ contents

Most enterprise AWS estates are not standalone. There is a data centre with a mainframe, a core banking system, an identity directory, or twenty years of file transfers, and AWS has to reach it. Two mechanisms.

The trap that catches people in interviews. Direct Connect is a private circuit. It is not an encrypted one. If your control requirement says "all data in transit must be encrypted" — and in banking it does — then Direct Connect alone does not satisfy it. You either run a VPN over the Direct Connect, or use MACsec where the connection supports it, or terminate TLS end-to-end at the application. Saying "we used Direct Connect so it's encrypted" is a wrong answer that sounds right, which is exactly what a good interviewer is fishing for.

SECTION 9DNS across the estate

↑ contents

DNS is the part that works perfectly in every demonstration and then breaks on the first day of real integration, when something in AWS tries to resolve a hostname that only the data centre knows about.

Route 53 private hosted zones hold names that exist only inside your network — payments.internal resolving to a private IP address. A private hosted zone is associated with one or more VPCs, and only those VPCs can resolve it.

For the hybrid case there are two pieces, and their names describe the direction of the query:

  • Resolver inbound endpoint — lets your data centre resolve names that live in AWS. Queries come in.
  • Resolver outbound endpoint, with forwarding rules — lets your AWS workloads resolve names that live in the data centre. Queries go out. The rule says "anything ending in corp.internal, ask these on-premises name servers".
   AWS                                          DATA CENTRE

SECTION 10The shared services account

↑ contents

Some things should exist once for the whole organisation rather than once per application. They live in the Infrastructure OU, usually split across a network account and a shared services account.

Lives centrallyWhy
The Transit Gateway and the VPC attachmentsOne hub, one set of route tables, one place where "prod may not reach dev" is enforced.
Outbound internet path (NAT, or a firewall)One place to inspect and log everything leaving the organisation. Also cheaper than a NAT gateway in every VPC.
DNS resolver endpoints and hosted zonesOne naming design, shared out. See Section 9.
Direct Connect and its gatewayThe circuit is a physical asset. It cannot sensibly belong to an application team.
CI/CD, container registry, golden imagesOne pipeline and one approved base image for everyone, which is also how you get patching under control.

Central egress versus egress everywhere — the trade-off

Central egress: all outbound internet traffic from every account goes through one inspected path in the network account. You get one place to log it, one place to apply a firewall, and one set of source addresses a partner can add to their allow-list. You also get a single point of failure and a component every workload now depends on, plus Transit Gateway data charges on traffic that used to be local.

Egress per VPC: simpler, more resilient, no shared dependency. But inspection and logging are now in forty places, source addresses are unpredictable, and your NAT gateway bill is multiplied by the number of VPCs.

There is no universally right answer, and that is the point. Regulated organisations almost always choose central egress because the inspection and the audit trail are the requirement, and they accept the dependency and pay to make it resilient. Say which you would pick, and say what you are accepting.

The shared VPC pattern

A variation worth knowing: using RAM, a network team can own a VPC and share its subnets to other accounts. Application teams then deploy into subnets they do not own and cannot modify. The network team keeps control of routing, and the application accounts keep their own IAM, quotas and billing.

It is a good fit for organisations with a strong central network function and a lot of small applications. It is a poor fit where teams need to move fast and independently, because every network change becomes a ticket.

figure 7 — the account and network map; numbers match the walkthrough below

One partner payment, followed all the way down

9
THE PARTNER, ON THE INTERNET

The partner's system requests an OAuth token from your authorisation server using its client credentials, then calls POST /payments with that token and an idempotency key.

Boundary 1. Everything before this point is outside your control. The token identifies the partner's application — it is not a person, and it is not an AWS identity.
6
PARTNER-INGRESS ACCOUNT · API GATEWAY

WAF filters obvious abuse. API Gateway validates the token, applies the partner's throttle and usage plan, checks the request against the schema, and rejects anything malformed before it reaches your code.

This account holds nothing valuable. If it is compromised, the attacker is in a building with no money in it — which is the entire reason it is a separate account.
6
INGESTION SERVICE (FARGATE)

Runs with its own IAM role. Here the identity changes hands: the partner's OAuth identity stops at this service, and everything from here on uses the service's AWS role. The service is responsible for carrying the partner's tenant identity forward as data, so attribution is not lost.

Get this wrong and you have a confused deputy — a powerful service acting on a request without remembering whose request it was.
6
PRIVATELINK, NOT PEERING

The ingestion account reaches exactly one endpoint in the payments account. The two networks are never joined.

Boundary 2, and the design decision a security reviewer will focus on. Peering here would mean an internet-facing account has a route into the account holding the ledger.
5
PAYFLOW-PROD · ALB IN THE PUBLIC SUBNET

Terminates TLS and routes to the transactions service. "Public subnet" means it has a route to the internet gateway — it does not mean this listener is open to the internet, which its security group decides.

7
TRANSACTIONS SERVICE · PRIVATE SUBNET

No inbound route from the internet exists. It fetches the database credential from Secrets Manager and the encryption key from KMS through interface endpoints, so neither request leaves the VPC.

Without those endpoints, both calls go out through the NAT gateway to public AWS endpoints — encrypted, but off your network, and billed per gigabyte.
8
AURORA · ISOLATED SUBNET

The ledger write commits. This subnet has no route out at all — not even through NAT. A compromised database host cannot call an attacker's server, and that sentence is the control.

Boundary 3, and the strongest one in the design, because it is enforced by the absence of a route rather than by a rule someone could change.
3
NETWORK ACCOUNT · TRANSIT GATEWAY

The core banking notification travels over Direct Connect to the data centre. The Transit Gateway's route tables allow prod to reach shared services and the data centre, and never allow prod and non-prod to see each other.

Direct Connect is private, not encrypted. The requirement for encryption in transit is met separately — mutual TLS to the core banking endpoint.
1
LOG ARCHIVE ACCOUNT

Every API call in every account, plus VPC flow logs, lands here. The bucket cannot be modified or deleted, enforced by a service control policy that even the account's own root user cannot escape.

This is the evidence. If it can be altered by anyone who can reach the account, it is not evidence.

The three boundaries, and why they are the answer

A partner request crosses three boundaries before it reaches money. One: the internet into an account that holds nothing. Two: PrivateLink into the payments account, exposing one endpoint rather than joining two networks. Three: into an isolated subnet with no route out.

Each one is a different kind of control — an account boundary, a service boundary, and a routing boundary. That is what "defence in depth" means concretely, and describing it this way is far stronger than listing the services involved.

SECTION 13Ten interview questions, with model answers

↑ contents

Cover the answers, attempt each one aloud, then read. The model answers are written at the length you would actually speak — roughly forty-five seconds each.

1. Why would you use multiple AWS accounts instead of one?

"The account is the strongest isolation boundary AWS offers. It separates blast radius, service quotas, billing and IAM in a single move — everything smaller is a wall inside the same building. Service control policies also only attach at account or OU level, so if I need a hard ceiling I need an account to hang it on. The cost is that accounts must be created and governed automatically, which is why I'd use Control Tower rather than doing it by hand."

2. Why should nothing run in the management account?

"Because service control policies don't apply to it. Anything running there sits outside my strongest preventive control. It's also the account that can create accounts and alter the organisation, so it's the highest-value target in the estate. It holds billing and organisation administration only, with a very short access list and the root credentials in a safe."

3. What's the difference between an SCP and an IAM policy?

"An IAM policy is the only one that grants anything. An SCP only ever subtracts — it sets the maximum available permission for an account. An action succeeds only if the IAM policy allows it and the SCP doesn't deny it. And SCPs apply to the root user of a member account, which is what makes them a genuine ceiling rather than a default someone can switch off."

4. How would you stop developers deploying outside Australia?

"A service control policy denying every Region except ap-southeast-2 and ap-southeast-4, attached at the organisational-unit level. That's preventive — the API call fails. I'd avoid answering this with AWS Config, because Config is detective: the resource gets created and I find out afterwards. I'd still run Config alongside it, for the report."

5. What makes a subnet public?

"Its route table has a route to the internet gateway. There's no public checkbox. A private subnet routes outbound through a NAT gateway instead, and an isolated subnet has no outbound route at all — which is where I'd put a database, so a compromised host can't call out."

6. Your NAT gateway bill is enormous. What do you look at first?

"Whether traffic to AWS services is going through it. By default a private subnet reaches S3 over the public endpoint, through NAT, and you're charged per gigabyte. Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB are free and remove that entirely, so they go in every VPC as a matter of course. Then I'd add interface endpoints for the services we actually use — those do cost money, per availability zone, so they're a deliberate list rather than all of them."

7. Ten VPCs need to talk to each other. Peering or Transit Gateway?

"Transit Gateway. Peering isn't transitive, so full connectivity across ten VPCs is forty-five peerings and forty-five sets of route table entries, and it stops being maintainable well before that. Transit Gateway also gives me route tables at the hub, which is where I'd enforce that production and non-production can never reach each other. The trade-offs are per-attachment and per-gigabyte cost, and a shared component I now have to make resilient."

8. A partner needs access to one of your services. How?

"PrivateLink, so I expose one service rather than joining two networks. The consumer reaches a single endpoint and has no route to anything else, and overlapping address ranges stop being a problem. If I peered instead, I'd be telling a security review that an external party has a route into my network, which is a different and much harder conversation."

9. The requirement says all data in transit must be encrypted, and we have Direct Connect. Are we compliant?

"No. Direct Connect is private, not encrypted — those are different properties. I'd meet the requirement with a VPN running over the Direct Connect, or MACsec if the connection supports it, or end-to-end TLS at the application layer, which I'd usually want anyway. Private connectivity reduces exposure; it doesn't satisfy an encryption control."

10. How do you make Direct Connect resilient?

"Two connections at two different Direct Connect locations, so a single facility or carrier failure doesn't take the link down, plus a Site-to-Site VPN as a lower-bandwidth backup path. I'd also be clear with the business that this is a real cost and a real lead time — circuits take weeks to months — so it belongs in the plan early rather than being discovered during testing."

SECTION 14Four exercises

↑ contents

Draw these yourself before looking anything up. What good looks like tells you what the answer must contain, not what the answer is.

Exercise 1 · account structure

A general insurer moving forty applications to AWS

Three environments each. Two of the applications handle health data with stricter controls than the rest. There is a small platform team of five. Design the organisation: OUs, accounts, and the three guardrails you would apply first.

What good looks like: you organise OUs by the policy that applies, not by the business unit. You put the health-data applications in their own OU rather than relying on tags. You name Control Tower and Account Factory rather than describing account creation by hand. You can say why nothing runs in the management account.

Exercise 2 · address planning

A telco with an existing 10.0.0.0/8 estate on-premises

They want AWS connected to it, and they are acquiring a company next year whose addressing is unknown. Plan the address space and say how you would handle the acquisition.

What good looks like: you reserve a contiguous range for AWS out of what the data centre is not using, rather than starting at 10.0. You size generously and can say why efficiency is the wrong instinct. You name IPAM. For the acquisition you say PrivateLink or a NAT-based interconnect, because overlapping ranges cannot be routed and re-addressing someone else's estate is not a plan.

Exercise 3 · connectivity

Central egress, or egress in every VPC?

Twenty-five VPCs across fifteen accounts. Security want every outbound connection inspected and logged. The platform team is worried about a single point of failure. Make the call and defend it.

What good looks like: you pick one and name what you are accepting. You mention Transit Gateway data-processing charges on traffic that used to be local. You mention that a partner allow-listing your source addresses effectively decides this for you. You say how you would make the central path resilient rather than pretending it isn't a dependency.

Exercise 4 · the whole thing, timed

Whiteboard a landing zone in twenty minutes

"We're a mid-sized bank with no AWS presence. Design our landing zone." Twenty minutes, out loud, with a drawing. Set a timer.

What good looks like: you ask about data residency, the data centre and the regulator before you draw anything. You draw the OU structure before the network. You name three guardrails without being asked. You cover DNS, which almost everybody forgets. And you finish, which is most of the exercise — timed communication is the skill being measured, not completeness.

Bring exercise 4 to the Headmaster chat. It is the one that maps directly to the interview format, and it is the piece of your preparation that has never been assessed. Twenty minutes, your drawing, no preparation — I will review it the way a panel would.

GLOSSARYEvery term in this book, in ordinary language

↑ contents

Landing zoneThe account structure, network and guardrails that exist before any application does. Why it matters: in an enterprise engagement it is usually the first thing you are asked to design, before anyone has agreed what the applications are.
AWS OrganizationsThe service that groups AWS accounts under one management account for billing and policy. Why it matters: it is where the hard guardrails attach, so nothing else in this book works without it.
Organisational unit (OU)A folder of accounts inside an organisation. Policies attached to an OU flow down to every account in it. Why it matters: naming OUs after policies rather than departments is what stops the structure rotting at the next reorganisation.
Management accountThe account that created the organisation and where billing consolidates. Why it matters: SCPs do not apply to it, so nothing should run in it.
Service control policy (SCP)An organisation-level rule setting the maximum permissions for an account. It can only subtract, never grant. Why it matters: it is the only control that binds even a member account's root user — and it is preventive, unlike AWS Config.
Permission boundaryA ceiling attached to one IAM role, capping what that role — and any role it creates — may do. Why it matters: it exists for delegation, letting a team create their own roles without escalating their own privileges.
Preventive vs detectivePreventive stops the action (an SCP makes the API call fail). Detective reports it afterwards (AWS Config finds the non-compliant resource). Why it matters: offering a detective control as the answer to "how would you stop that?" is a common and costly interview mistake.
IAM Identity CenterHow people sign in to AWS, federated from the corporate directory such as Entra ID, receiving temporary credentials. Why it matters: joiners and leavers are handled by the process that already exists, and no long-lived credential is created.
Assume role / trust policyA role has two policies: one saying what it can do, and a trust policy saying who may become it. Why it matters: this is how cross-account access works without anyone sharing credentials, and CloudTrail records the original identity.
Confused deputyA trusted service being tricked into using its own high privileges on behalf of a caller who should not have them. Why it matters: it is why a third party's trust policy should require an external ID, and why an ingestion service must carry the caller's identity forward.
VPCYour own private network inside AWS: an address range, subnets, and rules about what can reach what. Why it matters: it is the unit most network decisions are made in.
CIDR blockA range of IP addresses written as 10.20.0.0/16. The number after the slash says how large the range is — smaller number, bigger range. Why it matters: two overlapping ranges can never be connected, and fixing that means re-addressing an environment.
IPAMVPC IP Address Manager — the AWS service that hands out non-overlapping address ranges across an estate and tracks what is used. Why it matters: it is the answer to "how do you stop two teams picking the same range?"
Route tableThe list of "traffic for this destination goes to that target" attached to a subnet. Why it matters: it is what makes a subnet public or private. There is no other setting.
Internet gateway (IGW)Attached to a VPC; a route to it is what gives a subnet two-way internet reachability. Why it matters: only public subnets route to it, which is the definition of public.
NAT gatewayLets resources in a private subnet start connections outward, while nothing on the internet can start one inward. Why it matters: one per availability zone or you couple two zones together — and it is charged per hour and per gigabyte, which is a common bill surprise.
Security groupA stateful firewall attached to a resource: allow traffic in and the reply is automatically allowed out. Can reference other security groups. Why it matters: referencing groups instead of IP ranges keeps the rule correct as the system changes.
Network ACLA stateless allow-and-deny list at the subnet level; you must permit the return traffic explicitly. Why it matters: it is a coarse backstop that survives a misconfigured security group, not your primary control.
VPC endpointA private path from your VPC to an AWS service. Gateway endpoints serve S3 and DynamoDB only and are free. Interface endpoints serve almost everything else, create a network interface in your subnet, and are charged per hour and per gigabyte. Why it matters: without them, traffic to AWS services leaves your network through NAT and you pay for it.
Endpoint policyA policy on a VPC endpoint restricting which resources are reachable through it — for example, only your own S3 buckets. Why it matters: it is a genuine data-exfiltration control, and it is widely under-used.
PrivateLinkExposes one service to a consumer, one-way, without joining the two networks. Why it matters: overlapping address ranges stop mattering, and "we published one endpoint" is a far easier security conversation than "we joined our networks".
VPC peeringJoins two VPCs so they can route to each other. Why it matters: it is not transitive, so it does not scale — ten fully-connected VPCs would need forty-five peerings.
Transit GatewayA routing hub that every VPC attaches to once. Why it matters: its route tables are where you enforce "production and non-production may never reach each other", centrally and in one place.
VPC LatticeService-to-service connectivity and access policy at the application layer, across accounts, without route tables. Why it matters: it is a different model from route-based networking, so adopting it is an architectural decision rather than a configuration change.
Direct ConnectA private physical circuit from your data centre into AWS. Why it matters: it gives consistent latency and bandwidth, takes weeks to months to provision, and is private but not encrypted — which is the trap in most interview questions about it.
Site-to-Site VPNAn encrypted tunnel to your data centre over the public internet. Why it matters: available in hours and always encrypted, but with the internet's variable latency — usually the backup path rather than the primary one.
Private hosted zoneA Route 53 DNS zone that only resolves inside the VPCs you associate it with. Why it matters: it is how internal names exist without being published to the world.
Resolver endpointsInbound lets the data centre resolve AWS names; outbound plus a forwarding rule lets AWS workloads resolve data-centre names. Why it matters: hybrid DNS is the thing that works in every demo and breaks on the first real integration.
Resource Access Manager (RAM)Shares AWS resources — subnets, Transit Gateways, hosted zones — across accounts in an organisation. Why it matters: it is what makes central ownership of the network possible without central ownership of everything.
Control TowerSets up and governs a multi-account environment: the Security OU with Log Archive and Audit accounts, a control baseline, and Account Factory for new accounts. Why it matters: it is roughly the first three months of a hand-built landing zone, available on the first afternoon.
Blast radiusHow much breaks, and how many customers notice, when one thing fails or is compromised. Why it matters: it is the first and best argument for more than one account.
